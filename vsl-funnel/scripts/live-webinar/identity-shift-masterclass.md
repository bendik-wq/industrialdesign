## [00:20] Why acquisitions

[S20: "Elements of a deal": the business at the top, three lines down to "You", a pile of money, and "Seller".]

Let me show you what a deal actually is, because almost everyone pictures it wrong.

At the top, the business. Underneath it, three things. You. The seller. And a pile of money.

Look at where the money is. (pause) It's not on your side. It's not in your pocket. It sits in the middle, between you and the seller.

Most people think a deal works like this: you bring the money, the seller hands over the business. So the first question they ask is "how much have I got?", and when the answer is "not enough", they stop. [paradigm shift 1 opened]

That's not how it works. A deal is three parties and a pile of money, and your job as the buyer is to arrange where that money comes from. It can come from a lender. It can come from the seller, as a note paid over time or as a rollover, where they keep a stake. It can come from an investor. And it gets paid back from the profit of the business you're buying. Your own savings are one possible source among many, and usually the worst one.

Write this down: you don't need your own capital. You need to know how to arrange other people's. [paradigm shift 1 stated]

[S21: "Asset Types. Business Assets. Real Estate. Stocks. Bonds."]

[S22: "Business acquisitions is the only vehicle that can pay via: 1. Cash at closing 2. Cash flow / profit 3. Equity."]

And business acquisitions are the only one that can pay you three ways.

One, cash at closing. When a deal is structured well, the lender, the seller's note and the seller's rollover together can cover more than the price, with a working capital line on top so the business has cash in the bank on day one and you're not reaching into your own account to settle. You'll see clients on later slides who "overfinanced". That's what it means. And it's still debt, so it still has to pass the test I'm going to show you.

Two, cash flow and profit, every month, from the day you own it.

Three, equity. The value of what you own, which grows as you pay down the debt and grows again when the business becomes part of a group.

Most assets pay you one way, maybe two. A business you bought well pays you three.

A business is valued at its profit times a number, and the number depends on what kind of business the buyer thinks they're getting.

Same profit. (pause) Different multiple. And the second business was paid for by the lender, the seller's note and the seller's rollover. Your original business went up in value the moment it became part of a group.

## [00:44] C2: Capital

[S48: "The 2 core financing mechanisms: Vendor finance / rollover. Commercial lending."]

[S53: "The most important deal structuring principles. 1. free cash flow > debt service. 2. minimum 1.5 DSCR."]

[SLIDE: S51 "Vendor rollover"]

The seller keeps a stake in the business after the sale. Say ten or twenty percent, for illustration. They sell you the majority, they get paid for that through the bank and their note, and they keep a piece of what it becomes.

Yearly profit of the business you're buying, divided by the yearly payments on all the debt used to buy it. Bank loan, seller note, any working capital line. You want about one and a half.

- This is coaching and education, not financial, legal or tax advice. Say so once per intervention.
- They can stop any time. If they go quiet or say no, thank them and move on. Never push someone who says they can't afford it.
- End every intervention with a step they can take this week *whether or not they buy*. Then bridge once to the panel for the room, not for them.

### Intervention 1. Reframe a limiting belief (3 minutes)

**When:** someone types a "can't": "banks won't lend to me", "I'm not a deal guy", "sellers won't finance".

**Script**

"[Name], you wrote '[their words exactly]'. Can I use that live? [Wait for yes.]

Let me ask you something. Is that true for *every* bank, or is it true of the one conversation you had? [Let them answer.]

[margin: Meta-model challenge to a universal quantifier. "Banks won't" becomes "one bank didn't".]

So one bank, on one structure, said no. What did they actually object to: the profit, the paperwork, or you? [Let them answer.]

[margin: chunking down. A global belief broken into a specific, fixable part.]

Right. Here's the reframe. That bank didn't reject you. It rejected a structure. And structures can be rebuilt: more seller note, more rollover, a longer term, a different lender. You weren't told no. You were told *which part* to fix.

[margin: meaning reframe. Same event, new meaning: from verdict to instruction.]

And look at the evidence you already have. You built a business doing over a million a year. Nobody does that by stopping at the first no. So what would the version of you that built that business do with this 'no'?

[margin: counter-example from their own history; Sleight of Mouth 'apply to self'.]

This week: write down the three things that bank said, and next to each one, the lever that answers it. That's yours whether you work with us or not."

**Bridge to the room:** "If you've got your own version of '[their words]' in your head, that's exactly what the team works through with you. Panel's open."

### Intervention 2. The 1.5x test on their numbers (4 minutes)

**When:** someone types a real-ish profit and price, or says they ran the calculator.

**Script**

"[Name], you put in [profit] a year in profit and a price around [price]. Can we do yours live? [Wait for yes.] These are your rough numbers, so everything I say is for illustration, and it's not financial advice.

Let's say, for illustration, the bank funds seventy percent, the seller carries a twenty percent note and rolls ten percent. [Use the room calculator; read its output aloud.] The yearly payments come to about [X]. Your profit is [profit]. [Profit] divided by [X] is [ratio].

[margin: pacing with their own numbers. People believe their own arithmetic more than any claim.]

[IF IT PASSES] That clears one and a half. That business pays its own debt with [buffer] a year to spare. How does that land, seeing it on your numbers? [Let them answer.]

[IF IT FAILS] That's under one and a half. So which lever first: a longer note, a payment holiday on the note, or more rollover? [Let them choose; rerun once.] There. [new ratio]. That's how a deal gets *built*, not found.

[margin: yes-set and convincer strategy. Each step is something they agree is true, and they see it, hear it and say it.]

And notice what just happened. Two minutes ago that was a business you wondered about. Now it's a deal with a shape.

[margin: identity-level summary. "Wondered about" (owner) to "a deal with a shape" (buyer).]

This week: get three years of that business's real financials, or the closest you can, and run it again."

**Bridge:** "Everyone else: your numbers deserve the same two minutes. The calculator's there, and the engagement is that, every week, on real deals. Panel."

### Intervention 3. "Who is your first acquisition target?" in 2 minutes

**When:** someone types "not sure what I'd buy" or picks "Not sure yet" in the poll.

**Script**

"[Name], you said you're not sure what you'd buy. Can I give you two minutes on that? [Wait for yes.]

Three questions, fast. Who do you lose customers to? [Answer.] Who do you buy the most from? [Answer.] Who serves your customers right before or right after you do? [Answer.]

[margin: chunking down from the abstract 'a business' to named categories.]

Now, of those, which one is run by someone over sixty who's been at it twenty years? [Answer.]

[margin: presupposition. The question assumes such an owner exists and that they can name them.]

So when you buy [their answer], what changes first for your business? [Let them answer, in their words.]

[margin: future pace with 'when', not 'if'. They describe the post-acquisition state from inside it.]

That's your first target. Not a name yet, a type, in a market you already know better than any fund does. This week, write down five businesses that fit it."

**Bridge:** "If you can do that in two minutes live, imagine twelve weeks with a team building the list with you. Panel."

### Intervention 4. The board-in-a-week plan (4 minutes)

**When:** someone says "I don't have the credibility", "who'd lend to me", or asks about C1.

**Script**

"[Name], you asked about credibility. Can we build your week live? [Wait for yes.] To be clear: this is a plan for the week, not a board in a week. Jy's board took about a month. [VERIFY: S29/S46 says 29 or 30 days]

Day one. Write down three gaps a lender would see in you: finance, your industry's regulation, M&A. [Let them name them.]

[margin: chunking down. 'Credibility' turned into three named seats.]

Day two. On LinkedIn, list ten people who've done each of those for twenty years and are semi-retired. Day three and four. Send the board outreach message, the template from the slide. Day five. Two calls booked.

[margin: future pace in calendar time. Concrete days make it a behaviour, not a wish.]

What's the selling point for them? Not money. A seat, equity in what you're building, and a reason to stay in the game. [S40/S41: shares at a zero cost basis, in hope of profits, bonuses and exit value.]

And when those three names are on your deck, who walks into the lender's office? [Let them answer.]

[margin: Dilts' logical levels. Environment (LinkedIn), behaviour (messages), capability (board), identity ('the one who walks in').]

This week: days one and two. That's it."

**Bridge:** "The board message template is part of what you get with us. [CONFIRM BEFORE GOING LIVE: template bonus] Panel."

### Intervention 5. The partner conversation (3 minutes)

**When:** someone types "need to talk to my wife / husband / business partner".

**Script**

"[Name], you said you want to talk to your [partner]. Good. Can we prepare that conversation live, so it's an easy one? [Wait for yes.]

Sit in their chair for a second. What's the first thing they'll worry about? [Let them answer: usually money, risk, or time.]

[margin: perceptual positions. Second position: they step into the partner's view.]

That's fair. So give them the answer to that one first. If it's money: it's not your savings buying the business; it's the business's profit, through the bank, the seller's note and the rollover. If it's risk: the 1.5x test, and walking away from anything that fails it. If it's time: the seller stays through the handover.

And then the guarantee, word for word, with its conditions. Don't sell it. Show it.

[margin: matching the partner's criteria. You're not persuading the attendee; you're helping them be understood.]

Last thing. Ask them, 'What would you need to see to feel good about this?' and listen. If the answer's no, that's an answer.

This week, or tonight: have that conversation with the numbers in front of you."

**Bridge (gentle, once):** "The offer panel closes at the time shown on it. That's the honest window for that conversation."

### Intervention 6. "What would the buyer version of you do this week?" (3 minutes)

**When:** late in the Q&A, with someone who's engaged but stuck, or as the last intervention before the close.

**Script**

"[Name], can I ask you a slightly different question live? [Wait for yes.]

Picture yourself three years from now. You've bought two or three businesses. You run a group. The board meets every quarter. You're not in the middle of anything anymore. [pause] Got him? [Let them answer.]

[margin: Swish-style self-image. Build the compelling future self in detail before asking anything of it.]

Now, that version of you looks back at this week. What did he do *this week* that made the rest possible? [Let them answer. Wait. Silence is fine.]

[margin: the 'as if' frame and new behaviour generator. They borrow the future identity's judgement.]

What else? [One more.]

So who's already doing that? [pause] You just told me. You knew it. You've known it all night.

[margin: identity installed through their own words: Dilts' identity level reached from behaviour.]

This week: do the first thing you said. Write it in the chat so you've told the room."

[CHAT PROMPT for everyone: "What would the buyer version of you do this week? Type one thing."]

**Bridge:** "[ANCHOR] Owners build. Buyers compound. If what you typed needs a team behind it, the panel's open."

*Appendix notes:* run at most two interventions per showing, three if the chat is lively. Pick the person whose question the most other people would recognise. Interventions 2 and 3 sell hardest; 5 builds the most trust. Never run Intervention 5 as a close on someone who has said the answer at home is no.
