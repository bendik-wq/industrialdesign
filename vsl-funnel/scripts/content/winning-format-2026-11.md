# The next winning format for G&L (November 2026)

Prepared 2026-11-05 from 705 videos pulled through Monid: the most viewed YouTube results, TikToks and Instagram Reels for 7 in-niche queries (buy a business, business acquisition, seller financing, roll up strategy, how to buy a small business, private equity small business, buying a business with no money) and 7 outside-niche queries (business breakdown, how much does a business make, founder story, business owner advice, how rich people think about money, real estate deal breakdown, CEO explains). 14 winners were pulled with full transcripts. Source files: `scratchpad/winning/ranked.json`, `scratchpad/winning/selected.json`.

Instagram's search for "private equity small business" returned off-topic viral reels (cleaning hacks, coffee, a Pringles factory: a median of 20.2M views for that query). They are excluded from every number below.

**What this is:** a set of hypotheses with evidence, not proof. The data is a snapshot of top search results, views are lifetime totals at pull time, "outlier" (views divided by followers) exists only for TikTok, and the samples behind some patterns are small. The 4-week test plan at the end is how we find out.

- **YouTube rewards long.** The in-niche winners run 13:46 (Biaheza), 15:02 (Heacock), 24:43 (Codie), 40:54 (Modern MBA) and 43:02 (Jed Morris). Outside the niche, Simon Squibb's 2 h 26 min video has 18.1M views.

### What is saturated

- **"You don't need to start a business, just buy one" aimed at employees.** Heacock (1.71M) and Codie Sanchez (636,199) use near-identical titles; urfavcashflowcoach ("Why start a business when you can buy one?", 304,839) and rich.somers ("Best Small Businesses To Buy To Quit Your 9-5", 454,991) say it again in short form. The 75 titles in the set with job-escape language (quit, 9-5, corporate, salary, employee) have a median of 5,213 views.
- **"No money down."** 26 titles, 25 of them in-niche, median 32,123 views.
- **Generic roll-up explainers.** 94 titles mention roll-ups or private equity, median 2,088 views. The 30 TikToks for "roll up strategy" have a median of 602 views; only the one that did the arithmetic on screen (guildshore.ai, 80.7x) broke out.

### The gap, and why it matters for G&L specifically

Almost all niche content speaks to someone who doesn't own a business yet. That audience shows up in G&L's own funnel: of the 90 owners who booked calls, 63 were under $1M revenue. Content built on the saturated angles will keep pulling that audience.

Nobody in the 705 videos is making **deal content from the seat of an operator buying a competitor**: the lender's maths on a real-looking deal, the owner-replacement line only an operator understands, why the seller trusts the competitor down the road, and what two businesses are worth as one group. The pieces that work (numbers on screen, verdicts, PE follow-the-money, failure, series) exist; nobody has assembled them for the $1M+ owner.

## 2. The next winning format

### PRIMARY: "The 1.5x Test" (a recurring deal-scoring series)

**One line:** every episode, Josh takes one owner-led business that is (or looks like) a real listing, scores it the way a lender will with the 1.5x scorecard on screen, gives a verdict (pass, restructure or walk), and shows why the best buyer is the owner of the competitor down the road.

**Hook formula** (numbers in the first two seconds, then a yes/no question a lender answers):
- "[Trade] company: $[profit] profit, $[price] ask. Should its competitor buy it?"
- "A [trade] company makes $[profit]. The owner wants $[price]. Would a bank fund it?"
- Always followed within the beat by "illustrative" or "per the public listing, unverified".

**Structure, long form (8 to 12 min, YouTube), beat by beat, with the Studio layout:**

| # | Beat | Layout |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cold open: the deal in one line, the question, the promise (pass, fix, who should buy) | cinema |
| 2 | Call-out: for owners doing $1M+; "if you don't own one yet, this will feel early" | spotlight |
| 3 | The two slow ways past a revenue ceiling (organic hires; save or raise, then bid at auction) | spotlight |
| 4 | A real booking-form quote ("stuck at the 5-6m range need more locations") | orbit |
| 5 | The third way: commercial debt + seller finance + seller rollover | spotlight |
| 6 | The 1.5x test defined (and why we hold 1.5x when many lenders' floor is lower) | split |
| 7 | The deal card (listing screenshot or illustrative card) | stage |
| 8 | Adjusted profit is the broker's number: what the lender verifies | split |
| 9 | The owner-replacement line: cash available for debt | split |
| 10 | The typical first-time-buyer stack and its annual payments | split |
| 11 | Verdict 1 (usually a narrow fail) | spotlight |
| 12 | The order: seller first, lender second | spotlight |
| 13 | The restructure at the same price; verdict 2 | split |
| 14 | Mid-video soft CTA (apply link; live session) | orbit |
| 15 | Why a seller accepts rollover (paid twice) | spotlight |
| 16 | The seller's chair: why the competitor's owner is the safe buyer | orbit |
| 17 | Off-market: the best version never lists | spotlight |
| 18 | The group line: combined profit, 3-5x vs 8-12x (illustrative) | split |
| 19 | The walk-away rules that override a pass | spotlight |
| 20 | Josh's true proof (two acquisitions, 100% seller financed) | spotlight |
| 21 | Desire stack and the specific cost of waiting | spotlight |
| 22 | Close and CTA | spotlight |

**Short form (45 to 60 s; TikTok, Reels, Shorts; every beat vertical):** five beats. Deal and question (0 to 3 s) → owner replacement → stack and payments → verdict → cliffhanger ("part two fixes it at the same price") + CTA. Every deal ships as a two-part pair: part 1 fails, part 2 restructures, posted 24 hours apart. Pairs follow the "Part 2" pattern that gave liv_inmadrid 41% engagement.

**Mid form (3 to 4 min; YouTube, LinkedIn, Facebook):** one deal, three possible structures on screen, Josh picks one and explains why.

**On-screen device: the 1.5x scorecard.** A persistent overlay that becomes the series' visual signature, shown in split and stage beats:

```
THE 1.5x TEST                          [ILLUSTRATIVE] or [PER LISTING #___, UNVERIFIED]
Revenue ........................ $
Adjusted profit (verify) ....... $
Minus owner replacement ........ $
Cash for debt .................. $
Price / multiple ............... $  /  x
Stack  [ bank ███ | seller note ██ | rollover █ ]
Annual debt service ............ $
Coverage  ──────────|1.5x──────  1.47x
VERDICT:  PASS  /  RESTRUCTURE  /  WALK
Group line: combined profit $ · single 3-5x vs group 8-12x (illustrative)
```

The gauge needle crossing (or missing) the 1.5x line is the moment every episode builds to: it is the "count the quarters" moment from the laundromat reel, done as lender maths.

**Episode engine (how to make 50):**
- **10 owner-led trades** where consolidation is live: HVAC, plumbing, electrical, dental, veterinary, physio and allied health, imaging and radiology, pest control, landscaping, accounting and bookkeeping firms (spares: auto repair, childcare, funeral homes).
- **5 verdict archetypes**: clean pass; fail fixed by rollover; fail fixed by the seller note's terms or a payment holiday; walk because profit leaves with the owner; walk because of customer concentration or books that don't match the tax returns.
- 10 × 5 = 50 episodes, each cut into one long or mid video plus a two-part short pair.
- **Deal sources, in order of preference:** (a) real public listings from business-for-sale marketplaces, named on screen with the marketplace, listing ID and date, asking figures "per the listing, unverified"; (b) illustrative builds, labelled on screen and in speech; (c) deals viewers submit, only with written permission and anonymised. Never "a client of ours".
- **Comment engine:** end shorts on "Would you buy it?" (the HVAC reel's close). Deals viewers ask for in the comments become future episodes.

**Why it converts $1M+ owners specifically:**
1. It casts the viewer as the buyer who already owns a business. The owner-replacement line, shared dispatch, the lender who knows your numbers and "the seller trusts the competitor" only make sense to an operator. An employee watching gets nothing actionable, which is the point: the call-out filters in the first ten seconds.
2. It speaks to the booking-form pain directly ("stuck at the 5-6m range need more locations") and to the outcome they want: a group that trades at a higher multiple, being the buyer instead of the add-on.
3. The content is a demo of the service. The scorecard is the work G&L does on a strategy call, so the CTA ("want your own target scored?") is the natural next step, not a pivot.
4. It shows the method's two hardest ideas (the 1.5x test and the order) through a deal instead of a lecture, which is the "teach the what and why" principle from the live webinar brief.

### SUPPORTING FORMAT A: "Who Owns Your Street" (private equity follow-the-money, from the owner's seat)

- **Hook formula:** "Private equity [did X to industry Y]. Your [trade] is next." or "In every roll-up there are two kinds of owner: the buyer and the add-on."
- **Structure:** the industry that got rolled up (credited public reporting, e.g. Modern MBA on gyms) → the playbook in three moves (platform, add-ons on debt against their cash flow, exit at a group multiple) → the two seats (buyer or add-on) → not-your-fault: three things funds know (structure, the 1.5x test, the order) → the owner's edge (knows the trade and the off-market owners) → a true booking-form fact → Josh's proof → CTA.
- **Lengths:** YouTube long 12 to 20 min (Modern MBA shows 40 min works; start shorter on a new channel), mid 3 to 4 min, short 45 to 55 s.
- **Device:** the "seat chart" (buyer vs add-on) and the multiple ladder (single 3-5x → group 8-12x, illustrative).
- **Layouts:** spotlight for the thesis and the seats; stage for the credited source; split for the playbook and the multiple ladder; orbit for the booking-form fact.
- **Engine:** one consolidating industry per episode (gyms, vets, dental, HVAC, car washes, funeral homes, accounting, childcare, pest control, physio...), always with credited public sources.
- **Why it converts:** it turns the owner's background fear (a fund is buying my competitors) into the reason to act now, with a specific cost of waiting that is real, not invented.

### SUPPORTING FORMAT B: "The Deal That Died" (autopsy)

- **Hook formula:** "The seller said yes. Months later the bank said no. Here is the autopsy."
- **Structure:** the timeline of failure (find → meet → price → yes → legal and fees → the bank kills it) → why (coverage under 1.5x after owner replacement; the seller promised all cash) → the worse version (it closes anyway, on a personal guarantee) → a credited public story where one exists (Jed Morris, "Bankrupt at 40"), without diagnosing what we can't see → the order and the test → the $5.5M radiology deal with no personal guarantee → a booking-form quote ("I've acquired 10 clinics but some haven't made money") → Josh's proof → CTA.
- **Lengths:** mid 3 to 4 min (primary), short 45 s cutdowns, an occasional long autopsy.
- **Device:** the timeline bar that turns red at the break point, with the coverage gauge underneath.
- **Engine:** one failure mechanism per episode (owner-dependent profit, the all-cash seller, the wrong order, customer concentration, books vs tax returns, a personal guarantee, overpaying at auction, no transition plan), each paired with credited public stories when they exist.
- **Why it converts:** a $1M+ owner has the most to lose from a bad first deal. Fear of the wrong deal is a bigger blocker for them than access to deals, and the autopsy answers it.

**Kill and scale rules (review at the end of weeks 2 and 4):**
- **Scale:** the format with the highest $1M+ applications per 10,000 views gets double cadence, even when another format gets more views. Views without $1M+ applications is the trap the saturated content falls into.
- **Reshape:** a format with strong hold (above the bars) but no applications keeps its body and gets a new CTA beat and call-out.
- **Kill:** a format below the hook and hold bars on 4 consecutive posts, with a $1M+ share at or below the 30% booking-form baseline, stops. Its slot goes to the leader.
- **Hook test:** within the 1.5x Test, A/B the two hook formulas ("Should its competitor buy it?" vs "Would a bank fund it?") across pairs and keep the winner.

## Appendix: the six scripts, readable


### Script 1: Should You Buy Your Competitor? An HVAC Deal Through the 1.5x Test

- **Studio row:** format `long`, icp `ceiling`, framework `third_way`; 1316 words, est. 526 s
- **Topic:** The 1.5x Test, episode 1: should the owner of a $1M+ HVAC company buy the competitor down the road? An illustrative $3.2M deal scored line by line, failed, restructured and passed.
- **Hooks:** (0) An illustrative HVAC deal: $800K profit, $3.2M price. Should its competitor buy it? [chosen] / (1) Banks don't reject buyers. They reject structures. Watch one deal fail, then pass. / (2) Most owners stuck at $3M open another location. The owners building groups buy the competitor.
- **Titles:** Should You Buy Your Competitor? An HVAC Deal Through the 1.5x Test / This $3.2M HVAC Deal Fails the Bank Test. Here's the Fix. / How a $1M+ Owner Buys the Competitor Down the Road
- **Thumbnail:** 1.47x FAILS

**Beat 1 · cinema** · on screen: "The 1.5x Test: episode 1"

> An illustrative HVAC deal: $800K profit, $3.2M price. Should its competitor buy it?
> Today I'm running it through the 1.5x test, line by line, the way a lender will.
> By the end you'll know whether it passes, how to fix it when it doesn't, and why the best buyer for this business is the owner down the road, not the first-time buyer.

*Cue:* Measured, direct to lens. Small pause after the question.  
*Screen:* Title card: the empty scorecard, every row blank.

**Beat 2 · spotlight** · on screen: "For owners doing $1M+"

> This series is for owners of businesses doing a million or more in revenue who want to grow by buying, not by adding more of their own hours.
> If you're still looking for your first business to own, this will feel early for you. Everything here assumes you already run one.

*Cue:* Calm call-out. Lean in slightly on "already run one".

**Beat 3 · spotlight** · on screen: "Two ways. Both slow."

> There are two ways owners try to get past a revenue ceiling.
> The first is organic: another van, another location, another manager to hire and train. Growth arrives one hire at a time, and most of it still runs through you.
> The second is the money route: save profit for years, or raise equity and give someone a seat at your table. Then you bid on a listed business against private equity, at full price, through a broker running an auction.

*Cue:* Count the two ways on your fingers. Slower on "at full price".

**Beat 4 · orbit** · on screen: ""stuck at the 5-6m range""

> One owner wrote this on our booking form: stuck at the 5-6m range, need more locations.
> That sentence is the ceiling. And there's a third way past it that neither route touches.

*Cue:* Read the quote slowly, as written. Beat of silence after.  
*Screen:* The booking-form line, anonymised, on a plain card.

**Beat 5 · spotlight** · on screen: "Debt + seller note + rollover"

> The third way: buy a competitor and let the business you're buying carry its own price.
> Commercial debt, lent against the target's cash flow instead of your savings. Seller finance, where the seller is paid part of the price over time. And seller rollover, where the seller keeps a slice of the new company and gets paid a second time when the group sells.
> Whether that stack works comes down to one number.

*Cue:* Steady. One hand for each piece of the stack.

**Beat 6 · split** · on screen: "Cash flow / debt service: 1.5x"

> The 1.5x test.
> Take the cash the business produces in a year. Divide it by every debt payment due that year, the bank and the seller note together.
> At 1.5 or higher, the deal carries its debt with room for a bad quarter. Under 1.5, it doesn't, whatever the seller or the broker says.
> Many lenders set their floor lower than that. We hold the line at 1.5x, because a deal that only clears the floor has nothing left when a big customer walks.
