## [28:00] The test every deal has to pass

[SLIDE: The 1.5x test]

You've heard no money down pitches before, and most of you filed them next to get rich quick schemes. Most of them deserve it, and they fail for the same reason every time.

Somebody piles debt onto a business that can't carry it. The deal closes, the first months go fine, and then the payments come due and the business can't make them. So the buyer starts feeding it from their own pocket, and the no money down deal ends up costing them a great deal of money.

There's one number that stops that from happening, and commercial lenders have used it for decades. It's called debt service coverage.

[ON SCREEN: Debt service coverage = yearly profit ÷ yearly debt payments. The line: about 1.5x.]

You take the yearly profit of the business you're buying, before interest and tax. You divide it by the yearly payments on all the debt used to buy it: the bank loan, the seller's note, and any working capital line on top.

At about one and a half times, the business earns roughly a dollar fifty for every dollar it owes each year. It pays its own debt and keeps a buffer for a bad month, a lost customer or a slow season.

[SLIDE: Banks don't reject buyers | They reject structures]

This is the belief I most want to break tonight.

Owners tell me banks won't lend to them for an acquisition. The bank wants collateral they don't have, or wants them to put in half the price, or just says no.

What the bank is really telling you is that the structure in front of them doesn't work. A commercial lender looks at the cash flow of the business being bought, the quality of its financials, and whether the buyer knows how to run it.

Bring a lender a business that clears one and a half times, financials that match the tax returns, and a buyer who already runs a profitable company in the same industry, and you're in a very different conversation.

Banks don't reject buyers. They reject structures.

[SLIDE: What a lender reads]

So it's worth knowing what a lender reads when your deal lands on their desk.

They start with the profit over the last three years, and they want it steady or rising. Then they check whether those financials match the tax returns, because a profit that only exists in a spreadsheet counts for nothing.

After that, they look at how much of the price they're being asked to carry. When the seller carries a note behind the bank and rolls part of the price into equity, the bank funds a smaller share, and the seller has shown they believe in the business.

And last, they read you. A buyer who already runs a profitable business in the same industry is the lowest-risk buyer a lender sees. That's the reason the engagement is for owners above a million a year. Your own business is part of the case you bring to the lender.

[SLIDE: Worked example | Illustrative only. Education, not financial advice]

Here it is in numbers. Everything on this slide is illustrative. It's education, not financial, legal or tax advice, and every real deal gets structured on its own numbers.

Say a competitor makes five hundred thousand dollars a year in profit, and you agree a price of one and a half million. The bank loan and the seller's note together cost three hundred thousand dollars a year in payments.

[ON SCREEN: Illustrative. $500,000 ÷ $300,000 = 1.67x. Passes.]

Five hundred thousand divided by three hundred thousand is about one point six seven. That clears the line comfortably. The business pays its own debt, with two hundred thousand a year left over.

Now push the payments up to three hundred and fifty thousand a year, because the term is shorter or the price is higher. Five hundred divided by three fifty is about one point four three, and that fails. On paper it still makes money, but the buffer is too thin, and a lender sees that in about ten seconds.

[ON SCREEN: Illustrative. $500,000 ÷ $350,000 = 1.43x. Fails.]

[SLIDE: When a deal fails the test | Pull a lever, or walk away]

A failed test doesn't kill the deal on the spot. It tells you which lever to pull.

One lever is stretching the seller's note over more years, so each payment is smaller. Another is a payment holiday on the note for the first months while the business settles in. A third is moving more of the price out of debt and into the seller's rollover, which carries no yearly payment at all. And failing those, you renegotiate the price.

If none of that gets the deal over the line, you walk away before you've signed anything. Walking away from a deal that fails the test is the most profitable decision a buyer ever makes.

[SLIDE: "I've acquired 10 clinics but some haven't made money."]

Come back to the owner who wrote that on the form.

That owner already knew how to buy. What was missing was a filter at the front door. A test that screens out the businesses that can't pay their own way, before anyone signs.

The businesses that pass the test pay for the next one. The ones that fail it eat the profit of everything around them.

And here's the fourth reason the engagement exists. Running this test properly, on real financials, with the levers pulled in the right order, is where first-time buyers get it wrong. [SLIDE: No money down, done properly]

So when someone tells you no money down is a gimmick, they're half right.

No money down on a business that fails this test is a gimmick, and a dangerous one. No money down on a business that clears it is how commercial lenders, sellers and funds have bought businesses for decades, because each of them is betting on the cash flow of the business you're buying, and not on your savings.
