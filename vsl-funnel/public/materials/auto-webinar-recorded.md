## [19:00] The 1.5x test

[SLIDE: The 1.5x test]

You've heard no money down pitches before, and you've probably filed them next to get rich quick schemes. Most of them deserve it, and they fail for the same reason every time.

Somebody piles debt onto a business that can't carry it. The deal closes, the first few months go fine, and then the payments come due and the business can't make them. So the buyer starts feeding it from their own pocket, and the no money down deal ends up costing them everything they had.

[19:36]

There's a single number that stops that from happening, and lenders have used it for a long time. It's called the debt service coverage ratio.

[ON SCREEN: Debt service coverage ratio = yearly profit ÷ yearly debt payments. Target: about 1.5x.]

You take the yearly profit of the business you're buying, and you divide it by the yearly payments on all the debt used to buy it: the bank loan, the seller's note, and any working capital line on top.

At about one and a half times, the business earns roughly a dollar fifty for every dollar it owes each year. It pays its own debt and keeps a buffer for a bad month, a lost customer or a slow season.

[SLIDE: Banks don't reject buyers | They reject structures]

And this is the belief I most want to break in this hour.

Owners tell me banks won't lend to them for an acquisition. They'll say the bank wants collateral they don't have, or wants them to put in half the price.

What the bank is really telling you is that the structure in front of them doesn't work. A commercial lender looks at the cash flow of the business being bought, the quality of its financials, and whether the buyer knows how to run it. Bring them a business that clears one and a half times, financials that match the tax returns, and a buyer who already runs a profitable company in the same industry, and you're having a very different conversation.

[21:14]

Banks don't reject buyers. They reject structures.

[SLIDE: What a lender reads]

So it's worth knowing what a lender reads when your deal lands on their desk. First comes the profit, measured before interest and tax, over the last three years, and they want it steady or rising. Next is whether those financials match the tax returns, because a profit that only exists in a spreadsheet is no profit at all.

Then there's how much of the price they're being asked to carry. When the seller carries a note behind the bank and rolls part of the price into equity, the bank funds a smaller share of the deal, and the seller has shown they believe in it. And last, they read you. A buyer who already runs a profitable business in the same industry is the lowest risk buyer a lender sees, which is one more reason we only work with owners above a million a year.
