## [05:48] Why off market

[SLIDE: Why off market]

Start with where the deals are, and why we go off market.

A listed business is an auction. A broker has been paid to get the highest price, and the terms are set before you arrive. Every buyer in the market sees the same listing. The ones still there after a few months are either overpriced or have something wrong with them that other buyers already found.

An off-market deal starts somewhere else entirely. It starts with two owners talking about what each of them needs. There's no auction pushing the price up, and no broker setting the terms.

[ON SCREEN: Off market: no auction, no broker setting terms, two owners working out what each of them needs.]

That matters for the structure we're building this week. A seller note and a rollover are easy to agree when the seller is talking to the person who'll run their business. They're much harder to agree in an auction, where every bidder is being compared on the cash they put on the table.

[SLIDE: What listings are good for]

Listings aren't useless. They're just the wrong place to buy your first business. Use them for two things.

First, use them to learn prices. Read twenty listings in your industry and you'll see what sellers are asking, what multiples brokers quote, and how businesses describe themselves. That's free market research, and it makes you a better-informed buyer when you sit down with an owner who hasn't listed.

Second, use them to spot owners. A business that's been listed for a year without selling is an owner who wants out and hasn't found the right buyer. When the listing comes down, that owner is still there, and still wondering what to do. Put them on your list.

[SLIDE: An off-market deal is a fairer deal]

An off-market deal is also a fairer deal, for both sides. The seller gets a buyer who's thought about their staff and their name. The buyer gets time to understand the business properly. Neither of them pays a broker's fee on the whole price for a buyer they could have met directly.

And it's where the owner with no successor lives. They haven't listed, because listing makes it real. They're waiting, and the right approach from the right buyer is one of the things they're waiting for.

[SLIDE: "I've struggled to find a way to do this in aus with no money down"]

One owner who booked a call wrote: I've struggled to find a way to do this in Aus with no money down.

I'd bet part of that struggle came from looking at listed businesses. A listed business in an auction rarely gives you room for a note and a rollover, in Australia or anywhere else. The structure becomes possible when you're the one who started the conversation, with an owner nobody else has called yet.

[SLIDE: What not reaching out costs]

And let's be clear about what it costs to skip this. Every owner with no successor in your market is going to sell to someone, or close. If you don't approach them, the most likely buyer is a competitor who did, or a fund building a group in your industry.

When that happens, the business you could have owned is now owned by someone who'll compete with you for customers and staff, with more scale than before. The cost of not reaching out is the profit you didn't buy, plus a stronger competitor down the road.

[SLIDE: Who you're competing with]

You're not the only buyer calling these owners. Private equity funds and their analysts are building lists in fragmented industries everywhere. Some of them have teams whose only job is to call retiring owners every week.

Your advantage is that you're an operator in the same industry. Your disadvantage, today, is that you don't have a list or a rhythm. By the end of this session you'll have both.

## [10:03] Five places to find owners

[SLIDE: Five places to find owners who fit your buy box]

Here are the five places I'd look, in order. Most of them cost nothing but time, and the first three you already have access to.

[SLIDE: 1. Your own business]

The first place is your own business. Your senior staff hear everything. They know which competitor's owner keeps talking about retiring, which business has lost its best people, and whose kids have no interest in taking over.

Your customers know things too. In a lot of industries, customers use more than one provider, and they'll tell you which other business they used before you, and why.

Ask your leadership team one question this week: which owners in our industry do you think are thinking about stepping back? Write down every name.

[SLIDE: 2. Your suppliers]

The second place is your suppliers, and it's the one most owners overlook.

A supplier's sales rep visits every business in your industry in your region. They know who's growing, who's stopped ordering new equipment, who's late paying, and who's mentioned retirement over a coffee. They also want to keep selling to whoever owns those businesses next, so they have every reason to help a good operator buy one.

[ON SCREEN: Ask your suppliers' reps: which of your customers are winding down, and who would want an introduction?]

Next time a rep visits, ask them directly. Which of your customers are winding down? Would any of them want an introduction to someone who'd keep the business going and keep buying from you?

[SLIDE: 3. Your industry]

The third place is your industry itself. Associations, conferences, regional groups, training events and the industry's online forums.

These are the rooms where owners talk to each other. The owner who's been coming to the same association dinner for twenty-five years is exactly the seller in your buy box. Go to the next event with a list of names you're hoping to meet, and the sentence you typed in the chat today.

[SLIDE: 4. The advisers who serve owners]

The fourth place is the advisers who serve owners: accountants, lawyers, bankers and financial planners who work with businesses like yours.

An accountant who looks after twenty small businesses in your industry knows which owners are thinking about succession, because those owners ask them about it every year at tax time. A good adviser won't share confidential details. What they will do is mention to a client that they know a credible buyer, and ask if the client wants an introduction.

Tell your own accountant and lawyer what your buy box is. Ask them who else in their network works with owners in your industry.

[SLIDE: 5. Public records and directories]

The fifth place is public records and directories, which is where you build the bulk of your list.

In Australia, company records show who the directors of a company are and how long they've been there. In the United States, state business registries show similar information for each state. Industry directories, licensing boards, maps and review sites tell you who operates where, how long they've been trading and roughly how big they are.

[ON SCREEN: Public records show directors and how long they've held the role. Directories, licensing boards and maps show who operates where.]

None of these sources tells you an owner wants to sell. What they give you is a list of every business that fits your buy box on paper, with an owner's name next to it. The first four places tell you which of those owners to call first.

## [15:17] The weekly rhythm

[SLIDE: The weekly rhythm]

Now the part that separates owners who do a deal from owners who talk about doing one. Outreach is a weekly rhythm, never a one-off campaign.

Here's the honest arithmetic. Most owners you contact won't reply the first time. Of the ones who do, most will say not now. A few will say let's talk, and a smaller number of those conversations will turn into a meeting, then an NDA and financials.

That's not a failure rate. It's how off-market buying works, and every buyer who does deals, including the funds, runs the same funnel.

[ON SCREEN: The rhythm we use: 25 owner contacts a week, every week, using the same scripts, tracked on one list.]

So the rhythm we use is twenty-five owner contacts a week. Every week. A contact is a message, a letter, a call, a follow-up or a meeting, with an owner on your list.

Twenty-five sounds like a lot. Spread over a week, it's five a day, and once your list and your scripts exist, five contacts take about thirty minutes. Owners in this room spend more time than that on email they don't need to read.

[SLIDE: Thirty minutes a day]

Here's what the rhythm looks like in a normal week, so you can see it fits around a business you're already running.

Monday, you add five new owners to your list from one of the five places, and send their first messages. Tuesday and Wednesday, you send the follow-ups that are due, the second and third touches. Thursday, you make the calls that came from introductions or replies. Friday, you update the list: who replied, what they said, and when to check back.

[ON SCREEN: A weekly rhythm. Mon: five new owners and first messages · Tue and Wed: follow-ups due · Thu: calls from replies and introductions · Fri: update the list.]

That's five contacts a day, around thirty minutes, at the same time every day. Owners who put it in the calendar like a meeting keep doing it. Owners who do it when they have a spare moment stop in the second week, because there's never a spare moment.

And it's the kind of work a good assistant can support. Researching owners, keeping the list up to date and preparing letters can be delegated. The conversations themselves should stay with you.

[SLIDE: Why every week]

The reason it has to be every week is timing. An owner who says not now in March can say let's talk in June, because something changed: a health scare, a key staff member resigning, a lease renewal, a bad year.

You can't predict which owner, or which month. What you can control is being the credible buyer who was already in touch when it changed. Owners sell to the person who was there when they were ready, and the weekly rhythm is how you make sure that's you.

[SLIDE: Two buyers, one market. A composite, for illustration only.]

Here's what the rhythm does over a few months. Two buyers, made up to make a point, in the same industry and the same city.

The first buyer sends a burst of messages in the first week, gets a couple of polite replies and a lot of silence, and decides outreach doesn't work. Six months later, he hears that the owner he most wanted to buy has sold to a fund.

The second buyer sends twenty-five contacts a week and never misses one. Most weeks feel like nothing happened. Then, around the third month, an owner who'd said not now in the first month calls back, because her practice manager just resigned and she's tired. The second buyer is the only credible operator she's heard from more than once.

[ON SCREEN: Composite. Same market, same owners. The difference is who was still in touch when the owner was ready.]

Same market, same owners. The difference is who was still in touch when the owner was ready. That's what the rhythm buys you.

[SLIDE: Follow-ups do the work]

Most of the replies come from follow-ups, not first messages. A first message that gets no answer isn't a no. It's an owner who was busy, or who read it and thought about it, and didn't get round to replying.

Plan three touches for every owner over about six weeks. The first message. A short follow-up two weeks later. And a third touch, by a different channel, a few weeks after that. If you still hear nothing, they go back on the list for another try in a few months.

## [21:30] The first message

[SLIDE: The first message]

Now the first message. It matters more than people expect, and most buyers get it wrong in the first line.

Here's the one word that kills a first message if it's in the opening line: "acquisition."

[ON SCREEN: The word that kills a first message in its opening line: "acquisition".]

When an owner who hasn't decided to sell reads "acquisition" in the first line, they file you with the brokers, the funds and the spam. The message is about a transaction, and they're not ready for a transaction. They're ready, at most, for a conversation about the future of their business and their people.

So the first message is short, it's from one owner to another, and it's about them.

[SLIDE: The first message, line by line]

Here's the structure, line by line.

Line one says who you are and how you're connected. "I run a physiotherapy group in the northern suburbs, and our shared rep at the equipment supplier mentioned your clinic." One sentence. It tells them you're an operator, local, and not a stranger.

Line two says something true and specific about their business. "You've built one of the most respected practices in the area, and I've heard your team talk about how long they've been with you." It shows you know who they are, and it isn't flattery if it's true.

[ON SCREEN: 1. Who you are and how you're connected · 2. Something true about their business · 3. Why you're writing · 4. A small, easy ask.]

Line three says why you're writing. "I'm building a group of clinics run by people who care about their patients and staff, and I'd value a conversation about what you want for the future of the practice, whenever that is." This is where your buy box sentence lives, in softer words.

Line four is a small, easy ask. "Would you be open to a coffee in the next few weeks? No agenda beyond getting to know each other." A coffee is easy to say yes to. A meeting about selling the business is not.

That's about seventy words. It doesn't mention price, doesn't say "acquisition", and doesn't ask them to decide anything. It asks for a coffee with a credible person in their own industry.

[SLIDE: Line three for the other kinds of target]

If your buy box points at something other than a competitor, line three changes shape. The rest of the message stays the same.

Writing to a supplier, line three talks about continuity. "We've bought from businesses like yours for years, and I'd value a conversation about what you want for the future of the company and the people who run it." You're the customer who understands why their business matters.

Writing to a customer, or a business that sells on what you make, line three talks about the shared market. "We serve a lot of the same people, and I'd like to understand how you see the next few years for your business."

And writing to an adjacent business, line three talks about the customers you share. "Our clients use both of our services, and I've always admired how you look after them. I'd value a conversation about the future of the business, whenever that's on your mind."

[ON SCREEN: Line three changes with the target. Supplier: continuity · Customer: the shared market · Adjacent: the customers you both serve.]

In every version, line three is about their future and the people they care about. Your plan stays in the background until they ask about it.

[SLIDE: What not to put in a first message]

Here's what stays out of a first message.

No price, and no valuation, because you don't know enough yet, and a number in a first message is either insulting or a trap you've set for yourself.

No financing details. The note and the rollover come later, in the second conversation, and I'll show you exactly how.

No long story about you. One line on who you are is enough. They care about their business, and the message should too.

And nothing that sounds like a template. If you send the same message to twenty-five owners a week, change line two every time. It's the line that proves you're writing to them.

[SLIDE: Practise line two]

Line two is the one that makes or breaks the message, so let's practise it together.

Think of the owner at the top of your list. What's one thing that's true about their business that you'd admire if you were honest? It can be how long their staff have stayed, the reputation they have with customers, a service nobody else offers, or how they handled a hard year.

Type it in the chat as line two, the way you'd write it to them. Don't include their name or their business name.

[NOTE: Give the room about sixty seconds. Read three or four aloud. Point out which ones are specific enough to prove the buyer knows the business.]

Listen to the difference between those. "You've built a great business" could be sent to anyone. "Your team has been with you longer than most clinics in the area have existed" could only be sent to one owner, and that owner will read the rest of the message.

[SLIDE: Which channel]

Then there's the channel. Use whichever gets read in your industry.

A letter on your business letterhead, posted to the owner by name, gets opened in most industries, because owners get very few personal letters. Email works when you can find the owner's direct address, and a message on a professional network works for owners who use one. A phone call works best when someone has introduced you.

And in person beats all of them when you can arrange it, at an industry event or through a shared contact. Use one channel for the first message and a different one for the third touch.

[SLIDE: The follow-ups]

The follow-ups are shorter still. The second touch, two weeks later, is two lines: "I wrote a couple of weeks ago about your clinic and the future of the practice. If a coffee would be useful at any point, I'd be glad to." That's it.

The third touch, by a different channel, adds one new thing: an article about your industry, a note that you'll be at an upcoming event, a mutual contact you've discovered. Something that gives them a reason to reply other than guilt.

[SLIDE: When they reply]

When they reply, they'll say one of three things.

"Not interested." Thank them, sincerely, and ask if you can stay in touch. Put them back on the list for a year from now.

"Not now, but maybe later." Ask when would be a good time to check back, write it down, and do it. This is the reply that becomes a deal more than any other.

"Let's talk." Book the coffee within a week, before life gets in the way.

## [34:28] The first conversation

[SLIDE: The first conversation]

The owner said yes to a coffee, and the next hour decides whether there is a second one.

The first conversation is not a negotiation. You're not there to talk about price, structure or timing. You're there to understand the owner, and to let them decide whether you're the kind of person they'd trust with their business.

[ON SCREEN: The first conversation: listen more than you talk · learn their story · understand what they want · leave them wanting a second conversation.]

Listen more than you talk. Ask them how they started the business, what they're proudest of, how the industry has changed, who their key people are, and what they want the next few years to look like.

Most owners have never been asked those questions by someone who understands the answers. When you ask them, and listen properly, you become the buyer they remember.

[SLIDE: Five questions for the first conversation]

Here are five questions I'd have in my pocket.

How did the business start, and what's changed since? Who are the people you couldn't do without? What would you want to be doing in three years' time? If you stepped back tomorrow, what would worry you most? And what would a good outcome look like, for you and for your team?

That last question is the one that matters most. The answer tells you what the seller needs, and the second conversation is built on it.

[SLIDE: What you're listening for]

While they talk, you're listening for three things.

Their timeline. Are they thinking about stepping back in the next year or two, or is it a distant idea?

What they're afraid of. Being taken advantage of, what happens to their staff, who they'll be after the sale, or not being paid. Most owners will tell you exactly which one, if you let them talk.

And the red flags from yesterday. One customer in every answer, a team that can't decide anything without them, books that are "a bit messy".

[SLIDE: How the first conversation ends]

End the first conversation with something small and specific. "I've enjoyed this. Can I come and see the business sometime?" Or: "Would it be useful to meet again in a few weeks and talk about what a transition could look like for you and the team?"

Don't push for financials in the first meeting. If it goes well, the second conversation is where you ask for an NDA and three years of financials, and the owner will be ready for it.

[SLIDE: What owners say in the first conversation]

Owners say the same handful of things in a first conversation, and it helps to have heard them before you hear them for real. Here are five, with how I'd answer each one.

[SLIDE: "I'm not ready yet."]

The first is "I'm not ready yet." That's the most common thing you'll hear, and it's good news. It means they've thought about it.

Answer it by agreeing. "That makes complete sense. Most owners I talk to aren't ready when we first meet. I'd just like to be someone you know when you are." Then ask what ready would look like for them. Is it a year away, three years, after a particular milestone? Write the answer down and put a date on your list.

[SLIDE: "My kids may want it one day."]

The second is "My kids may want it one day." Sometimes that's true, and you should respect it. Just as frequently, it's a hope the owner hasn't tested.

You can ask gently: "Have they worked in the business?" or "Have you talked with them about it?" If the answer is no, you've learned that the successor question is open. Don't push it. Leave the door open, and stay in touch.

[SLIDE: "What would you pay?"]

The third is "What would you pay?" in the first meeting. We'll cover this in the questions, but the short version is: you don't know yet, and you say so honestly. You'd want to understand the business before naming a number you can stand behind. Then ask what they'd hope for, and listen.

[SLIDE: "I'd want to stay involved."]

The fourth is "I'd want to stay involved." That's not an objection, it's an opening. A seller who wants to stay involved is a seller who'll carry a note, take a rollover and help with the handover.

Ask what involved means to them. A few days a week for a year? A role with key customers? A seat at the table for big decisions? Each of those has a place in a well-structured deal, and the seller who wants one is easier to work with than the seller who wants out on settlement day.

[SLIDE: "My staff will panic if they hear about this."]

And the fifth is "My staff will panic if they hear about this." They're right to worry, and you should say so.

Tell them confidentiality protects both of you, and that nothing reaches their team until the owner decides it should. Explain that when the time comes, you'd want to meet the key people with them, and tell the team together what stays the same. That answer, said calmly, does more for trust than anything else in the first conversation.

## [40:54] The second conversation: how you'd pay

[SLIDE: The second conversation | How you'd pay, from the seller's side]

Now the conversation every first-time buyer dreads. At some point, the seller will ask how you'd pay. And if the answer sounds like a buyer trying to avoid paying cash, the deal is over.

So we're going to look at this conversation from the seller's side of the table, because that's the only side that matters when you're having it.

[SLIDE: What the seller is really asking]

When a seller asks how you'd pay, they're asking four questions at once.

Will I actually get the money? Are my people going to be looked after? Does the business keep its name and reputation? And what's my role, if any, after the sale?

[ON SCREEN: "How would you pay?" really means: Will I get paid? Will my people be looked after? Will the business keep its name? What's my role after?]

A buyer who answers only the first question, with a number, misses three of the four. A buyer who answers all four, in the right order, is the buyer the seller chooses, even against someone offering more cash.

[SLIDE: Start with their people and their name]

So you start with the three that aren't about money. Remind them of what they told you in the first conversation. "You said your biggest worry is your team. Here's what happens to them." Then tell them: who runs the business, who keeps their job, what stays the same for customers, and what happens to the name.

You can say all of that honestly, because it's what you'd do anyway. You're buying a business that works, from someone who built it well. Keeping the team and the name is how you protect what you're paying for.

[SLIDE: Then explain how they get paid]

Then you explain how they get paid, in three parts, and each part answers a worry.

The first part is the money at settlement. Most of the price is paid on the day the deal closes, funded by a commercial lender who has tested the business's cash flow. That part answers "will I actually get the money".

The second part is the seller note. A share of the price is paid to them over a few years, with interest, out of the profit of the business they built. It sits behind the bank, and it means they keep earning from the business after they've stepped back.

The third part is the seller rollover. They keep a slice of the business, so they share in whatever it becomes as part of a bigger group. They get paid twice: once at settlement, and again when the group is sold or their stake is bought out.

[ON SCREEN: How the seller gets paid: most of it at settlement from a commercial lender · a share over a few years as a seller note · a slice kept as seller rollover, paid again later.]

[SLIDE: The seller's side, in numbers. Illustrative.]

Here's what that looks like from their side, with the illustrative deal from Day 1: a business making five hundred thousand a year, at a price of one and a half million.

There are good reasons, on the seller's side, to agree to a note and a rollover.

The rollover lets them sell the bulk of the business, step back from the day to day, and still share in the next chapter, at a group's value instead of a single business's.
