## [07:36] What a buy box is for

[SLIDE: What a buy box is for]

A buy box is a short written description of the business you'll buy. It's one page, it fits on a screen, and it tells you, your advisers and the owners you approach exactly what you're looking for.

[ON SCREEN: A buy box decides what you look at, who you call, and what you say no to]

It does three jobs. First, it decides what you look at, so you stop spending evenings on listings that were never a fit. Second, it settles who you call, so your outreach goes to the right owners. And it decides what you say no to, which is the job that saves you the most money.

Owners without a buy box look at everything. Every business for sale looks interesting, every conversation feels like progress, and six months later they've seen thirty businesses and made no offers.

Owners with a buy box look at far fewer businesses, and they make offers on the ones that fit. When a seller says, tell me what you're looking for, they can answer in two sentences.

[SLIDE: Why sellers and lenders care]

There's a second reason it matters. Sellers and lenders both read it.

A seller with no successor is working out if you're a serious buyer, or someone who will waste their time. When you can describe exactly the kind of business you buy, and theirs fits it, you sound like someone who has done the thinking. That's half of what makes a seller trust you.

A lender is deciding whether the deal makes sense for you. A target that sits squarely in your buy box, in your industry, at your capacity, with a manager in place, reads as a sensible next step. A target outside it reads as a bet.

[SLIDE: The buy box protects you from yourself]

And the third reason, which is the honest one. The buy box protects you from yourself.

Every buyer falls in love with a deal at some point. A beautiful business, a charming seller, a price that sounds like a bargain. The buy box is the thing you wrote down when you were calm, and it's there to argue with you when you're not.

One owner who booked a call with my team wrote: I've acquired ten clinics, but some haven't made money. I'd bet that at least some of those clinics would never have passed a written buy box. We'll come back to that owner in the red flags section, because the pattern behind that sentence is the most expensive mistake in this whole game.

Type in the chat: have you ever bought something for your business that you knew, deep down, didn't fit? Yes or no.

[NOTE: Read the rough split aloud.]

Thank you for being honest. Most owners have, and an acquisition is the most expensive version of that mistake. That's why we write the buy box before we look at a single business.

## [11:18] Four kinds of target

[SLIDE: Four kinds of target | Competitor · Supplier · Customer · Adjacent]

The first line of your buy box is the kind of business you'll buy. There are four, and you voted on them yesterday: a competitor, a supplier, a customer, and an adjacent business that sells something different to the same people.

Each one has a clear upside and a clear trap. I'll take them one at a time.

[SLIDE: 1) A competitor]

A competitor does the same work as you, for the same kind of customer, in a place or a segment you don't already reach.

The upside is that you understand the business completely. You know what good looks like, you can read their numbers and spot what's off, and you know how to run it from the first day. The seller sees you as the obvious successor, and a lender sees a buyer who already runs the same asset.

There's a second upside that owners love. Combining two competitors creates savings you can see on paper: one set of software, shared purchasing, one finance function, a single marketing budget doing the work of two. Those savings raise the profit of the combined group.

[ON SCREEN: Competitor · Upside: you know it, the seller trusts you, the savings are real · Trap: the overlap you were counting on isn't there]

The trap with a competitor is overlap. If the business sits right next to yours, some customers use both, and you can lose some of that revenue when they realise both businesses are now the same owner. And if you plan the deal around savings, check they're real before you count them, because savings on a slide are easier to find than savings in a payroll.

For most owners in this room, a competitor is the first deal I'd look at, and I'll come back to why in a minute.

[SLIDE: 2) A supplier]

A supplier sells you something you already buy. Equipment, materials, a service you outsource, a lab, a parts distributor.

The upside is margin. Today you pay that supplier a profit on everything you buy from them. When you own them, that profit stays inside your group. You also control quality, supply and price on something your business depends on.

[ON SCREEN: Supplier · Upside: you keep the margin you pay out today · Trap: their other customers leave once a competitor owns them]

The trap is the supplier's other customers. If your competitors buy from that supplier too, some of them will stop once they learn you own it. Suddenly the business you bought loses part of its revenue on the day the news gets out. So when you look at a supplier, read the customer list carefully, and ask how much of the revenue comes from businesses that compete with you.

A supplier also runs on a different operating model from yours. You know how to sell what they make, which isn't the same as knowing how to make it.

[SLIDE: 3) A customer]

A customer buys from you. In some industries, that's a business that sells on what you make, or uses your service as part of theirs.

The upside is demand. When you own a customer, you control a channel for your own product, and you can grow both businesses together.

[ON SCREEN: Customer · Upside: you control demand · Trap: you're learning a new business, and their suppliers include your competitors]

The trap is that you're learning a new business. A customer's operations, customers and risks are different from yours, and your industry knowledge helps less than it does with a competitor. They also buy from your competitors today, and those relationships change when you own them.

Owning a customer can be a great second or third deal. As a first deal, it asks you to learn the acquisition process and a new business at the same time.

[SLIDE: 4) An adjacent business]

An adjacent business sells something different to the same people you already serve. A physiotherapy group buying a sports medicine clinic. An HVAC business buying a plumbing business. An accounting firm buying a payroll provider.

The upside is the customer base. You can offer your services to their customers, and theirs to yours, and the combined business is worth more to each customer than either was alone.

[ON SCREEN: Adjacent · Upside: you sell more to the same customers · Trap: you don't know the operations, and cross-selling takes longer than the slide says]

The trap is the same as with a customer, in a milder form. You know the customers, but you don't know the operations. And cross-selling, which looks automatic on paper, takes real work and real time to happen. Don't put it in the price you pay, and don't count on it in your test.

[SLIDE: Which one first?]

So which first? For most owners in this room, I'd start with a competitor, or a business so close to a competitor that you'd run it with the same playbook.

The reason is risk. On a first deal you're learning the process: the outreach, the structure, the lender, the letter of intent, the handover. You want everything else about the deal to be familiar. A competitor is the business you already know how to run, which leaves your attention free for the parts you don't.

Suppliers, customers and adjacent businesses can be excellent second and third deals, once you've done one and know how the process feels.

[SLIDE: The business you already have in mind]

Some of you already have a specific business in mind. You've driven past it for years, or you've heard the owner wants out, or you buy from them every month.

That's useful, and it's also a trap. A business you've already picked tends to write your buy box for you. You set the size line to fit it, you decide the geography is fine, and you find reasons the owner's dependence on one customer doesn't matter.

So here's the discipline. Write your buy box tonight as if that business didn't exist. Then hold the business up against it, line by line. If it fits, you've got a strong first target and a clear reason for it. If it doesn't, you've just saved yourself from the most common first-deal mistake there is.

## [19:08] The owner with no successor

[SLIDE: The owner with no successor]

The next part of your buy box is the most important, and it's not about the business at all. It's about the owner.

The best deals sit with an owner who hasn't listed yet. Plenty of them never will.

That owner has been running the business for twenty or thirty years. The kids have their own careers. The senior staff can run the floor but can't buy the business. And the owner knows, somewhere in the back of their mind, that they're going to have to do something about it.

[ON SCREEN: The owner with no successor: years in the business · no family to take over · staff who can't buy it · a growing sense that something has to happen]

Every industry has a generation of owners in that spot right now. If your industry has small, profitable, owner-run businesses, it has this seller, probably within an hour of where you're sitting.

[SLIDE: Why the listed market is the wrong place to look]

Most owners who decide to buy start by opening a business-for-sale website. They scroll through the listings and come away thinking the good deals are all taken.

About the listings, they're right. A listed business is an auction. A broker has been paid to get the highest price, every buyer in the market can see it, and the businesses that are still there after a few months are picked over or priced for a buyer with a fund behind them.

The owner with no successor isn't on those sites. They haven't called a broker, because calling a broker makes it real, and they're not ready for that. They're waiting for something, and the right buyer turning up is one of the things they're waiting for.

[SLIDE: Four signs an owner is ready]

You can spot them before they've told anyone. There are four signs, and you can find most of them without speaking to the owner at all.

The first is tenure. The owner has run the business for a long time, and they started it or took it over a generation ago. In most places, company records will tell you how long the same person has been a director.

The second is the absence of a successor. No family member in the business, no partner who's younger, no obvious heir among the staff. Ask around your industry and you'll hear it quickly.

The third is the energy of the business. The website hasn't changed in years, the marketing has gone quiet, there's no new location, no new service. The business is still profitable, but the owner has stopped pushing.

The fourth is what the owner says. They mention retirement to suppliers. Some complain about regulations, staffing or the paperwork. They talk about their grandchildren more than their plans. People in your industry hear it, and so do your staff.

[ON SCREEN: Four signs: long tenure · no successor · a business that's stopped pushing · an owner who talks about stepping back]

Type in the chat: can you name one owner in your market who shows at least two of those four signs? Just type yes or no. Don't type their name.

[NOTE: Read the rough split aloud.]

I'm reading a lot of yes answers. Hold that owner in your head. On Day 3 they go on your list, and I'll show you exactly what to say to them first.

[SLIDE: Why they'd sell to you]

Now the part people get wrong, which is why that owner would ever sell to you, on terms that include being paid over time.

An owner with no successor has two problems. Someone has to pay them, and someone has to look after the staff, the customers and the name they spent their life building.

The note and the rollover solve the first problem. The seller keeps earning from the business after the sale, out of its own profit, and with a rollover they share in whatever it becomes.

You solve the second problem by being who you are. You already run a profitable business in the same industry. To that owner you look like the obvious successor, someone who'll keep the team on, look after the customers and keep the name alive.

[SLIDE: What the owner is afraid of]

It helps to understand what that owner is afraid of, because every fear is a line in your eventual offer.

They're afraid of being taken advantage of. They've heard stories about buyers who promised the world, paid a fraction and fired half the staff. So the first thing they're judging is whether you're someone who keeps your word.

They're afraid of what happens to their people. Some of their staff have been with them for fifteen years. The owner feels responsible for those jobs, and a buyer who talks about cutting costs in the first meeting loses that owner on the spot.

They're afraid of losing their identity. For thirty years they've been the person who owns that business. Selling it raises a quiet question about who they are on the Monday after settlement, and a role in the transition, or a rollover stake, answers part of that question.

[ON SCREEN: The owner's fears: being taken advantage of · what happens to their people · who they are after the sale · not being paid]

And they're afraid of not being paid. Which is why they'll ask how you'd pay, and why the answer has to make sense from their side of the table.

Notice that only one of those four fears is about money. A buyer who walks in talking only about price is answering one question out of four. On Day 3, we'll write a first message and plan a second conversation that answers all four, in the right order.

[SLIDE: Private equity is calling them too]

You're not the only buyer who's noticed these owners. Private equity funds buy small, profitable businesses in fragmented industries and stitch them together into groups. They use the same structure you're learning this week: debt against the business's cash flow, a seller note and a slice of rollover.

A fund will send an analyst. You turn up as an operator who has done the same work for years and will still be in the industry long after the papers are signed. To a seller worried about their staff and their name, that's the difference that decides who they sell to.

But the fund has one thing you don't have yet: a list, and a process to work it every week. That's what Day 3 gives you.

[SLIDE: The owner as a buy box line]

So here's how the owner becomes a line in your buy box. Write down the owner situation you're looking for. For example: owner over sixty or in the business more than fifteen years, no family successor, open to staying on for a handover.

That one line tells you who to approach first, and it tells you what to listen for in the first conversation.

## [27:02] Building your buy box

[SLIDE: Your buy box | Eight lines on one page]

Now let's build the whole thing. Your buy box has eight lines. You'll fill them in tonight on the worksheet on your day page, and I'm going to walk through each one with an illustrative example.

[ON SCREEN: 1) Industry · 2) Kind of target · 3) Where · 4) Size · 5) Owner situation · 6) Who runs it · 7) Financial standards · 8) Deal-breakers]

Line one is the industry. Be specific. "Healthcare" is too broad, while "allied health clinics" is useful and "physiotherapy and exercise physiology clinics" is better. The narrower the line, the easier it is to build a list of owners and the more credible you sound when you call them.

Line two is the kind of target: competitor, supplier, customer or adjacent. For a first deal, I'd write competitor unless you have a strong reason for something else.

Line three is where. A geography you can actually manage. Close enough to drive to, or in a city where you already have staff, suppliers or customers. A business four hours away is a business you'll visit less than you planned.

[SLIDE: Line four · Size, from your capacity number]

Line four is size, and it comes straight from last night's homework. Write the profit range of your first target and the illustrative price that profit can carry on the test.

For example: target profit of three to six hundred thousand dollars a year, price up to about three point three times profit on a seventy, twenty, ten structure. That one line rules out most of the businesses you'd otherwise waste time on.

Add a revenue range if it helps your search, because owners and databases talk in revenue more than profit. But the profit line is the one that decides whether the deal passes.

[SLIDE: Lines five and six · The owner, and who runs it after]

Line five is the owner situation we just covered. Long tenure, no successor, open to a handover.

Line six is who runs the business after the seller steps back. This is the line most first-time buyers leave out, and it's the one that decides how much of your week the deal will eat.

There are three answers. A manager is already in place and runs the business day to day. A senior staff member can step up with support. Or the owner does everything, and you'll need to hire or move someone in. For a first deal, I'd want one of the first two.

[ON SCREEN: Line 6) Who runs it after? A manager in place · a senior person who can step up · nobody yet]

If the answer is nobody, the deal can still work, but your capacity number from last night shrinks, because part of it now goes on running the business yourself.

[SLIDE: Line seven · Financial standards]

Line seven is your financial standards. These are the minimums a business has to meet before you'll look closely.

Three years of financial statements that match the tax returns. Profit that's steady or rising across those three years. No single customer making up more than a set share of revenue. Pick a number you're comfortable with, such as twenty percent, and write it down.

And the target has to pass the one and a half times test at a price inside your capacity. If it doesn't pass, and none of the Day 4 levers fix it, it's out.

[SLIDE: Line eight · Deal-breakers]

Line eight is your deal-breakers: the things that end a conversation, however good everything else looks. We'll build that list in the red flags section in a minute.

[SLIDE: An illustrative buy box]

Here's what a full buy box looks like, with illustrative details.

[ON SCREEN: Illustrative buy box · Physiotherapy clinics · competitor · within 90 minutes of our main site · $300K to $600K profit, price up to about 3.3x · owner 15+ years, no successor, will stay 6 to 12 months · senior clinician who can run it · 3 years of clean financials, no customer over 20% · walk away on any red flag]

Physiotherapy clinics. A competitor. Within ninety minutes of our main clinic. Three hundred to six hundred thousand dollars a year in profit, price up to about three point three times on our structure.

An owner fifteen years or more in the business, with no successor, willing to stay on for six to twelve months. A senior clinician who can run it day to day. Three years of clean financials, no customer or referrer over twenty percent of revenue. Any red flag ends the conversation.

That's it. When an owner asks what you're looking for, you read them that, and they know in thirty seconds whether their business fits.

[SLIDE: Say it out loud]

A buy box only works if you can say it out loud without reading it. Owners will ask you on the phone, at an industry dinner, in a car park after a meeting. If you have to think about the answer, you sound like someone who hasn't decided.

So try it now. In the Zoom chat, type your buy box as one sentence, the way you'd say it to an owner who asked what you're looking for. Don't worry about getting every line in. Industry, kind, where and size is enough for a first try.

[NOTE: Give the room about sixty seconds. Read three or four sentences aloud and tighten one of them live, cutting any word that doesn't help the owner decide.]

I'm reading a few. Listen to how much clearer the short ones sound. An owner hears "I buy physiotherapy clinics in the northern suburbs making three to six hundred thousand a year" and knows straight away whether that's them. An owner who hears "I'm looking at healthcare opportunities" hears every other buyer who has ever called them.

Keep tightening yours tonight. By tomorrow, the sentence you type in the chat becomes the second line of your first message to an owner.

[SLIDE: Three buy boxes · Composites, for illustration only]

To make this concrete, here are three buy boxes for three different owners. They're composites, made up to show how the same eight lines produce very different targets.

[ON SCREEN: Composite A · HVAC services, $6M revenue · Buys a competitor's residential service book in the next suburb over]

Owner A runs an HVAC service business doing six million a year in revenue. Their buy box says residential HVAC and plumbing service businesses, a competitor or a close adjacent, inside the metro area they already cover.

Size comes from their capacity number: two hundred and fifty to five hundred thousand dollars of profit. The owner they want has been running the business for more than twenty years, has no family in it, and has a service manager who dispatches the technicians. Their deal-breakers are a business that depends on one builder for most of its work, and a fleet of vans that needs replacing in year one.

Read that back and you can see the whole search. They know which owners to call, what to ask in the first meeting and what makes them walk away.

[ON SCREEN: Composite B · Accounting firm, $3M revenue · Buys a retiring partner's client book]

Owner B runs an accounting firm doing three million a year. Their buy box says small accounting practices with a retiring principal, a competitor, within the same state so the clients don't notice the change.

Their size line is smaller, a hundred and fifty to three hundred thousand dollars of profit, because the target's clients are attached to the retiring principal and the handover is the biggest risk. So line five matters more than anything else: a principal who'll stay for twelve months and introduce every major client personally. Their deal-breaker is a book where the top ten clients are more than half the fees.

[ON SCREEN: Composite C · Dental group, $14M revenue · Buys a single-site practice with an associate who wants to stay]

Owner C runs a dental group doing fourteen million a year across several sites. Their buy box says single-site general dental practices with a principal near retirement, a competitor, in suburbs next to their existing sites.

Their size line is bigger, five hundred thousand to a million in profit, and line six carries the deal: an associate dentist who already works there and wants to stay. Their deal-breakers are a lease with less than five years to run and equipment that needs a full replacement.

[SLIDE: What the three have in common]

Three industries, three sizes, three very different targets. What they share is that each buy box is narrow enough to build a list from, and each one has a line six that answers who runs the business after the owner steps back.

Notice too that each deal-breaker is specific to the industry. The HVAC owner worries about vans and builders, the accountant about client concentration, the dentist about leases and chairs. Your line eight should read like it was written by someone who knows your industry, because it was.

[SLIDE: Rebuilding the profit · Illustrative]

Before we get to the red flags, one more skill belongs in your buy box, because it decides whether a business really fits your size line. It's rebuilding the seller's profit into the number a lender will actually use.

Sellers show you their profit with add-backs. That means expenses that run through the business but won't continue after you buy it: the owner's car, a family member on the payroll who doesn't work there, a one-off legal bill. Adding those back is fair, and every buyer does it.

What sellers rarely do is take out the cost of replacing themselves. If the owner works full time in the business and pays themselves a small salary, someone has to do that job after the sale, and that someone costs money.

[ON SCREEN: Illustrative · Seller's profit with add-backs: $650,000 · Less a manager at a fair salary: $170,000 · Profit to test: $480,000]

Here's an illustration. A seller tells you the business makes six hundred and fifty thousand dollars a year, with their add-backs included. They work in it five days a week and pay themselves very little.

You'll need a manager to replace them. Say a fair salary for that role, with on-costs, is a hundred and seventy thousand dollars a year. Take it out, and the profit you're really buying is four hundred and eighty thousand.

Now run your capacity maths on the right number. Four hundred and eighty thousand divided by one and a half is three hundred and twenty thousand a year in debt payments. Times five, on our illustrative terms, is a price of about one point six million.

If the seller is asking two million because they think the business makes six hundred and fifty thousand, you now know why the price doesn't fit, and you can explain it calmly with their own numbers. That conversation is far easier at this stage than after a lender rejects the deal.

[SLIDE: Add-backs a lender cuts]

And one warning about add-backs. A lender will accept the ones they can verify from the records: a car, an insurance policy, a clearly personal expense. They'll cut the ones they can't. If a seller tells you half the profit is add-backs, ask for the paperwork behind each one before you count them in your test.

Type in the chat: does the owner of a business you're thinking about work full time in it? Yes, no, or don't know.

[NOTE: Read the rough split aloud.]

Thank you. If you typed yes or don't know, rebuild the profit with a manager's salary before that business goes anywhere near your size line.

That's why I push owners toward narrow buy boxes even when a broad one feels safer. A narrow buy box today is what lets you tell a buyer, years from now, that you own a group with a clear story and a management team, rather than a collection of businesses you happened to buy.

[SLIDE: What a missing buy box costs]

And let me put a cost on skipping this. Owners without a buy box spend months on deals that were never a fit. They take meetings, sign NDAs, read financials, run models and talk to lenders, and then walk away for a reason that would have been obvious on day one if they'd written it down.

## [46:44] Red flags

[SLIDE: Red flags]

Now the deal-breakers, the most valuable part of today. Every deal you walk away from at this stage costs you nothing. Every red flag you miss costs you money for years.

[SLIDE: "I've acquired 10 clinics but some haven't made money"]

Back to the owner who wrote: I've acquired ten clinics, but some haven't made money.

That's an owner who knows how to buy. They've done it ten times. What they didn't have was a door that some deals couldn't get through. Each of the red flags I'm about to give you is a reason a profitable-looking business turns out not to make money once you own it.

[SLIDE: Red flag 1) The financials don't match the tax returns]

Red flag one: the financials don't match the tax returns.

Sellers sometimes show you one set of numbers and file another. Sometimes it's an honest mess, and sometimes it isn't. Either way, a lender will rebuild the profit from what was filed with the tax office, and that's the number your test runs on. If the profit you're buying only exists in a spreadsheet, you're paying for something that isn't there.

[SLIDE: Red flag 2) One customer is most of the revenue]

Red flag two: one customer or one referrer makes up a large share of the revenue.

If a single customer is thirty or forty percent of the business, you're not buying a business, you're buying a contract that can end. When that customer leaves, the profit leaves with them, and your payments to the bank and the seller don't.

[SLIDE: Red flag 3) The seller won't stay for the handover]

Red flag three: the seller won't stay through the handover.

A seller who wants all cash and a clean exit on day one is telling you something. Either they know a problem is coming, or the business depends on them in ways you won't see until they're gone. A seller who trusts the business is happy to stay six or twelve months, and a seller with a rollover has every reason to.

[SLIDE: Red flag 4) The profit leaves with the owner]

Red flag four: the profit leaves with the owner.

Some businesses only make money because the owner works sixty or eighty hours a week and pays themselves almost nothing. When you replace the owner with a manager on a fair salary, the profit shrinks, or disappears. Always rebuild the profit with a fair wage for whoever will run it, and test that number, not the seller's.

[ON SCREEN: Walk away if: the financials don't match the tax returns · one customer is most of the revenue · the seller won't stay for the handover · the profit leaves with the owner]

Those four are the ones we walk away from every time. There are a few more I'd add to your deal-breaker list depending on your industry.

[SLIDE: More flags to check]

Key staff with no reason to stay. If two people hold all the relationships and neither has a contract or a reason to stay, they can leave with the customers.

Revenue falling for two or three years in a row. Steady or rising profit is a lender's first question for a reason.

A lease, licence or contract that ends soon or can't be transferred. A great clinic in a building you can't stay in is not a great clinic.

Equipment, vehicles or systems at the end of their life. If the business needs a large spend in year one, that cost belongs in your price and your test.

And add-backs that look too good. Sellers add back personal expenses to show you the real profit, which is fair. When the add-backs are bigger than the profit, be careful, because a lender will cut the ones they can't verify.

[SLIDE: Red flags are cheaper than levers]

Here's the rule I want you to write down. A failing test can be fixed with a lever, and on Day 4 I'll show you four. A red flag can't be fixed with a lever. It can only be avoided.

No amount of seller finance fixes a business that loses its biggest customer in month three. No payment holiday fixes a profit that only existed while the old owner worked for nothing.

[SLIDE: Listening for red flags in the first meeting]

You won't see most red flags in the first conversation, because you won't have the financials yet. But you can hear them, and it's worth knowing what to listen for.

When you ask the owner about their best customers, listen for how many names come up. If one name dominates the answer, ask how much of the business they represent. A pause before that answer tells you something.

When you ask about the team, listen for whether the owner talks about people who could run the place, or only about people who do the work. If every decision runs through the owner, line six of your buy box is already in trouble.

[ON SCREEN: In the first meeting, listen for: one customer in every answer · a team that can't decide without the owner · "I'd want to be out quickly" · "the books are a bit messy"]

And when you ask what they'd want for themselves after a sale, listen for how quickly they want out. A seller who says they'd want to be gone on settlement day is telling you the handover will be hard.

Finally, listen for anything about the books. "My accountant handles all that," said with a shrug, is fine. "The books are a bit messy, but the real numbers are better," is a phrase that's ended a lot of deals, and in plenty of first meetings it's the first red flag you'll hear. The second number is the one you can't verify, and the one a lender won't lend against.

None of these end the conversation on the spot. They tell you what to check first when the financials arrive, and they save you weeks.
