# G&L hook bank: buy growth (3C model)

For owners of profitable service businesses doing **$1M to $100M a year**. Everything here runs on the 3C Acquisition Model (Capabilities, Capital, Closing) and is written with the four-sales method (`ads/brief.md`): the hook makes the *content sale*, the copy block starts the *hope sale* by knocking out one old belief and installing a new one.

Deal maths uses the 3C structures with real arithmetic. Multiples and margins are the 3C model's illustrative ranges and are labelled that way on screen. History is sourced in the fact sheet at the end. † needs Josh's sign-off.

## Part 2 · The 3C maths

Three structures from the 3C model, run with real arithmetic. Rates: commercial debt at about 10% (prime is 7.00% as of November 2026), vendor notes at 4% (3C range 3–5%).

### The target used in A1 and A2 (illustrative)
A service business with **$3M revenue** and a **20% margin** (the 3C screen is 15%+): **$600K EBITDA**.

### A1 · 100% vendor finance with a payment holiday
The seller finances the whole price. 4% interest, 7-year term, and nothing paid for the first 12 months.

| Price | Year 1 payments | Years 2–7 payments | Cover | Left after debt (yrs 2–7) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **3x = $1.8M** | **$0** | $357K a year | **1.68x ✓** | $243K a year |
| 3.5x = $2.1M | $0 | $417K a year | 1.44x ✗ | $183K a year |
| 4x = $2.4M | $0 | $476K a year | 1.26x ✗ | $124K a year |

- **What it means:** at 3x the business pays for itself, and in year one all $600K stays in the business while you settle in. At 4x, the same deal fails the test. Same business, same seller, different price: structure is the whole game.
- **Your cash:** $0 into the price. Legal and accounting come out of a working-capital line drawn at settlement (3C sizes it at about 10% of revenue, so about $300K here).
- **Realism:** 100% vendor finance is the best case, not the default. It happens when the seller trusts the buyer, wants a clean handover and is paid a fair price over time. Josh's first two deals were structured this way.

### A2 · 60% commercial debt + 40% seller rollover
Same business at **4x = $2.4M**. The bank lends $1.44M against its cash flow (10%, 7 years). The seller keeps 40% of the business instead of cash for that part.

| | Per year |
|---|---|
| Bank repayments | $296K |
| **Cover** | **$600K ÷ $296K = 2.03x ✓** |
| Profit after debt | $304K |
| Your 60% | $183K |
| The seller's 40% | $122K |

- **What it means:** the bank validates the deal, the seller stays invested in the handover, and the seller is paid twice: once at settlement, again when the group sells. Your cash into the price: $0.
- **Realism:** a bank lends on this when the buyer has a track record and a credible team. An owner already running a profitable $1M+ business has exactly that.

### B · $10M–$100M operator: the roll-up
Buy **five businesses with $1M EBITDA each at 4x = $20M**, each on a 60/40 structure.

| | |
|---|---|
| Bank debt (60%) | $12M at 10% over 7 years = **$2.46M a year** |
| Acquired EBITDA | $5M a year |
| **Cover** | **2.03x ✓** |
| After debt service | $2.54M a year |
| Your cash into the price | $0 (sellers roll 40%) |

**The multiple arbitrage (3C illustrative ranges):** that $5M of EBITDA cost about 4x, or $20M. Inside a group valued at 8x it's worth **$40M**; at 10x, **$50M**. Size creates the difference, not hours.

**The 3C roll-up ladder (illustrative):** 10 businesses → $5–10M exit · 50 businesses → $40–80M · 100 businesses → $100–200M. Three to ten years from first deal to exit.

### Build vs buy (real anchors)
- A new dental practice costs **$500K to $950K** to open and takes **12 to 36 months** to reach profitability.
- An established practice sells for about **65–77% of one year's collections**, with patients, staff and profit from settlement day.
- Every new location you build runs at a loss until it doesn't. Every business you buy right pays its own debt from month one.

**Ad articulations:**
> Building a location means funding the fit-out, the equipment, the hires and the marketing, then a year or three of losses. Buying one means a structure. The customers, the staff and the profit arrive on settlement day, and the profit pays the purchase.

> The seller financed the whole price, and he didn't ask for a cent in the first twelve months. In year one, every dollar of profit stayed in the business.

> At four times earnings, the bank lends sixty percent against the business's own cash flow and the seller keeps forty percent. You put in nothing, and the business pays the bank twice over.

> Buy five companies at four times earnings. Put them under one roof. A group that size is valued at eight to ten. The difference is created by size, not by working harder.

## Part 3 · The hooks

Each: the line, the picture, the belief it shifts, and a copy block.

### Family 1 · History repeats (B1)

**H1 · Rockefeller**
- **Hook:** "In 1872, John D. Rockefeller bought twenty-two of his rivals in about six weeks."
- **Picture:** 1870s Cleveland in winter, smokestacks, a ledger with names crossed out one by one.
> He didn't out-refine them. He bought them.
>
> Within a decade, Standard Oil controlled about ninety percent of American refining.
>
> A hundred and fifty years later, private equity runs the same play on dental practices, HVAC companies and vet clinics.
>
> The owners still growing one customer at a time are the refineries in this story.

**H2 · Morgan buys Carnegie**
- **Hook:** "In 1901, J.P. Morgan bought Carnegie Steel and created the first billion-dollar company in history."
- **Picture:** a handshake over a contract; a steel mill at night.
- **Shift:** B1, B7.
> Morgan paid about four hundred and eighty million dollars, merged it with the steelmakers he already controlled, and the new company was capitalised at one point four billion.
>
> The same mills under one roof were valued at almost three times the price.
>
> That's the roll-up, and it works at every size. A group is worth more than its parts.

**H3 · Disney**
- **Hook:** "Disney paid four billion dollars for Marvel. Ten years later, Marvel films had grossed over eighteen billion."
- **Picture:** a cinema door opening onto a projector beam.
> Disney didn't try to invent a thousand superheroes. It bought the company that already had them.
>
> Then it did the same with Pixar and Lucasfilm.
>
> Your industry has a Marvel. The only question is who buys it.

**H4 · YouTube**
- **Hook:** "Google bought YouTube in 2006. Last year YouTube's ads alone brought in thirty-six billion dollars."
- **Picture:** a 2006 browser window, then a stadium screen.
> Google had its own video product. It bought the one people were already using.
>
> Building would have taken years, with no promise of catching up. Buying put Google in front of the audience the day the deal closed.

**H5 · Instagram**
- **Hook:** "Facebook bought Instagram for about a billion dollars. Seven years later, it was reportedly earning twenty billion a year."
- **Picture:** a phone lighting up in a dark room.
> Zuckerberg saw a competitor taking his users' attention, and he didn't try to out-build it. He bought it.
>
> Every industry has an Instagram: the competitor quietly winning the customers you want. In a service business, that competitor tends to be owned by someone close to retiring.

**H6 · Buffett's candy shop**
- **Hook:** "Warren Buffett paid twenty-five million dollars for a candy company in 1972. It has earned him over two billion since."
- **Picture:** a black-and-white chocolate box, a ledger ticking up.
- **Shift:** B1, B3.
> See's Candies made less than five million a year before tax when he bought it.
>
> He didn't open a candy store. He bought one that already had the customers, the recipes and the loyalty.
>
> Berkshire Hathaway was built by buying businesses that already worked. The businesses on your street work too.

**H7 · History repeats**
- **Hook:** "Rockefeller, Morgan, Disney, Google, Buffett. Every one of them grew the same way, and it wasn't marketing."
- **Picture:** five portraits, a deal stamp landing on each.
> They bought companies that already had the customers, the people and the profit.
>
> History keeps repeating it because it keeps working. The only thing that changes is the size of the cheque.

### Family 2 · The 3C mechanism (B3, B4)

**H8 · The payment holiday**
- **Hook:** "The seller financed the whole price, and didn't ask for a cent for twelve months."
- **Picture:** a calendar with twelve months crossed out, every page stamped "$0".
> That's a hundred percent vendor finance with a payment holiday.
>
> The seller is paid over time, out of the profits of the business they sold. In year one, every dollar of profit stays in the business while you take over.
>
> Josh Li's first two acquisitions were structured this way.

**H9 · Who pays**
- **Hook:** "Who pays for a business when it's bought right? The business does."
- **Picture:** a cheque signed by the business's own logo.
> The bank lends against the profit of the company you're buying.
>
> The seller finances part of the price and rolls part of it over.
>
> When that profit covers the repayments one and a half times, the business carries its own debt.

**H10 · The best lender**
- **Hook:** "Who's the best lender for your next acquisition? The person selling it."
- **Picture:** a retiring owner handing over keys and a promissory note.
> A seller who trusts you would rather be paid over time from a business that keeps running than wait years for a cash buyer who never comes.
>
> That trust is built before the offer, with a team and a track record behind you.

**H11 · Paid twice**
- **Hook:** "The smartest sellers don't cash out. They get paid twice."
- **Picture:** two cheques with the same signature, years apart.
> Seller rollover means the seller keeps a slice of the business they sold.
>
> They're paid once at settlement, and again when the group sells at a bigger multiple.
>
> For the buyer, it means sixty percent bank debt, forty percent seller equity, and none of your own cash in the price.

**H12 · Even the lawyers**
- **Hook:** "On a well-built deal, even the lawyers get paid out of the deal."
- **Picture:** an invoice sliding into a settlement folder.
> A working-capital line drawn at settlement covers the legal fees, the accountants and the runway.
>
> That's how serious buyers close without writing a personal cheque.

**H13 · Structure is the game**
- **Hook:** "The same business from the same seller pays for itself at three times earnings, and sinks you at four."
- **Picture:** two identical shopfronts, one lit, one dark.
> Finance a six hundred thousand dollar business over seven years at three times earnings, and it covers its repayments one point seven times.
>
> Pay four times, and cover drops to one point three. The bank says no, and it should.
>
> Banks don't reject buyers. They reject structures. *(illustrative)*

**H14 · Sellers sell to safety**
- **Hook:** "Sellers don't sell to the highest bidder. They sell to whoever makes them feel safe."
- **Picture:** an old owner at a kitchen table, reading an offer, glancing at a family photo.
> They're handing over thirty years of work, their staff and their name.
>
> A buyer with a chair, a finance lead, a lawyer and an operator behind them is the safest offer on the table, even when it isn't the biggest.

**H15 · A thousand calls**
- **Hook:** "Josh Li made about a thousand cold calls before his first deal closed."
- **Picture:** a call log filling a page at night, four lines struck through.
> Four deals died on the table. The fifth one was one hundred percent seller financed.
>
> The best businesses never reach a broker. They go to the buyer who asked first and kept asking.

**H18** · "You're paying retail for every customer. There's a wholesale price."

>

**H19** · "Building a new location costs a fortune before it earns a dollar. Buying one can pay for itself from the first month."
- **Picture:** a burning fuse on the left, a lit storefront on the right.

### Family 4 · $10M–$100M operators (B6, B7)

**H20** · "Private equity is rolling up your industry right now. In five years you're either the platform or the add-on."
- **Picture:** a regional map with pins flipping to one colour.

**H21** · "Buy at four times earnings. Get valued at eight. That's what private equity does with your competitors."
- **Picture:** two price tags on the same building, one small, one huge.
> Small businesses trade at three to five times earnings. Platforms of them trade at eight to twelve. *(illustrative ranges)*
>
> Buy five companies at four times, put them under one roof, and the same profit is valued twice over.
>
> Private equity built an industry on that gap. Owners your size are allowed to use it too.

**H22** · "Heartland Dental didn't build two thousand offices one at a time."
- **Picture:** a map filling with tooth icons.
> In 2025, one acquisition alone added sixty practices to its network.
>
> That's growth when it's bought instead of built. The same playbook works at your scale, with smaller cheques.

**H23** · "Someone is going to consolidate your industry. If it isn't you, they'll be the one making you an offer."
- **Picture:** an envelope under an office door: "Letter of Intent".

**H24** · "Ten businesses is a $5–10 million exit. A hundred is $100–200 million. The only difference is how many times you repeat one deal."
- **Picture:** one key, ten keys, a wall of keys. *(illustrative, from the 3C roll-up ladder)*

**H25** · "You have cash earning less than inflation and a market full of owners who want out. Those two facts should be talking to each other."

### Family 5 · Certainty and the clock (B8)

**H26** · "What are the odds your biggest competitor walks away one day? 1 in 1."
- **Picture:** a shop sign being unscrewed.
> Every owner stops. They sell, hand it down or shut the doors.
>
> McKinsey expects about six million small and mid-size businesses to change hands by 2035 as owners retire.
>
> Their customers will go to whoever asks first.

**H27** · "Every business you drive past today will have a new owner one day."
- **Picture:** a dashcam drive down a main street, signs passing.

**H28** · "You will sell your business one day. That's guaranteed. The only question is whether you sell one company or a group."
- **Picture:** one key, then a ring of keys.

### Family 6 · The second one is easier (B5)

**H29** · "The hardest business you'll ever run is your first one. You've already done that part."
- **Picture:** a battered first set of keys beside a clean second set.
> The second business inherits your systems, your suppliers, your finance team and the managers you trust.
>
> Your buying power goes up the day it settles, and your overheads spread across two sets of revenue.

**H30** · "If revenue drops every time you take a week off, you own a job. A group runs without you."
- **Picture:** an empty office chair on a Tuesday, the phone still ringing.

### Family 7 · Owners already doing deals (B9)

**H31** · "If your last acquisition hasn't paid for itself yet, the business wasn't the problem. The structure was."
- **Picture:** a spreadsheet, one row turning red.

**H32** · "We met an owner with dozens of sites whose last ten acquisitions all lost money." †
- **Picture:** ten rows in red.
> Two things broke them, and neither was the price.
>
> The debt was sized to the purchase instead of the profit, and the sites ran on the owner instead of a system.
>
> Fix those two, and an acquisition carries itself from the first month.

**H33** · "If you're putting your own cash into every deal, you're doing acquisitions the expensive way."
- **Picture:** a personal chequebook slid back across a deal table.

### Family 8 · The disqualifier as a hook

- **Picture:** a velvet rope.

**H35** · "If you run a $10 million business and you're still growing it one customer at a time, this will annoy you."
- **Picture:** a stopwatch next to a sales funnel.

## Part 5 · Rules

- Multiples (3–5x, 8–12x), margins and roll-up exits are the 3C model's illustrative ranges: label them on screen.
- "No money down" is never promised. Say the deal "can be structured" with none of your own cash, and show the test it must pass.
- Funding always names the pieces: commercial debt against the target's cash flow, vendor finance, seller rollover.
- Only Josh's approved proof; † items need his sign-off.
- Box office is "grossed", never profit. Instagram's 2019 revenue is "reportedly".
- No fake deadlines. The real clock is retiring owners and consolidation.
