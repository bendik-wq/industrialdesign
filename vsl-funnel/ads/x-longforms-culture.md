# X long-forms: "Performance = Culture" (aggressive cut)

Built on Josh's call with a lender funding a PE-backed healthcare roll-up. Details anonymised: no company, lender, fund or GM is named or identifiable.

## A1 · Where's the money?

A private-equity fund bought a healthcare roll-up, hired a GM on a monster salary, and ran the place on vibes.

Four clinics have already closed.

The fund is tapped out. The lender is now the only thing keeping the lights on.

I spoke to that lender today. He told me he sat in on one of their leadership meetings and nearly walked out.

Values, mission statements and uplifting energy: a whole hour on culture.

"The whole time I'm thinking," he said, "where's the money?"

Read that again, because this is the most expensive mistake in business and almost every owner is making a smaller version of it right now.

They think culture creates performance.

It's the other way round. Performance creates culture.

Nobody feels inspired working for a business that's closing sites. Nobody believes the mission statement when the payroll is late. Win, and the culture builds itself. Lose, and no workshop on earth will save you.

Now here's the part that should scare you if you run a $1M to $100M business.

That roll-up had everything you think you're missing: private-equity money, a big team, multiple sites and an expensive executive.

And it's dying, because it bought businesses without knowing how to run them, and it priced the debt like the good years would never end.

Most owners look at acquisitions and think the hard part is the money.

The money is the easy part.

My first deal, at 25, unemployed, after about a thousand cold calls: 100% seller financed, 7 years at 4%, a 9-month payment holiday, no personal guarantee, no security. In year one it paid me about $150k in distributions. It has roughly tripled its revenue since.

My second: 100% seller financed again, and a couple of months ago it moved into a facility three times the size.

I didn't do that because I'm a genius. I did it because before I made a single offer, I built a board of people who'd actually run healthcare businesses. Operators, not cheerleaders.

That's the whole difference between the roll-up that's closing clinics and the one that's opening them.

Structure the deal so the target's profit covers the repayments one and a half times. Put people around you who've run the thing you're buying. Measure performance from day one and let the culture follow it.

Here's what doing nothing costs you.

Every year you stay at one business, private equity buys another one of your competitors, the retiring owner down the road sells to whoever asked first, and the multiple you'll be bought at gets set by somebody else.

And here's what doing it wrong costs you: four closed clinics, a tapped-out fund, and a lender in your boardroom asking where the money went.

You can't afford either.

This is for owners of profitable businesses doing $1M+ a year who want to buy their way to the next level and want it done properly: the structure, the board, the integration, the first 100 days.

If you're under that, don't DM me. Grow the core business first.

If you're over it, DM me "OPERATOR" and tell me what you do and what you're doing in revenue.

## A2 · Your culture deck is a cope

Tell a team their culture is amazing and they clap.

Show them the business closed four locations this year and the room goes dead silent.

I just watched this happen at a private-equity-backed healthcare roll-up. Big salaries. Bigger hype. A GM paid a fortune to run culture sessions while the numbers bled out. The fund behind it is out of money and the lender is now paying the bills.

If you own a business, you're doing a smaller version of this right now.

You're running another ad when the problem is the offer, hiring another manager when the problem is the system, and working 70 hours a week because "that's what it takes," while a competitor buys a whole company's revenue in one signature.

Call it hard work if you like. It's cope.

Performance is the culture, revenue is the morale, and cash flow is the only team-building exercise that works.

The fastest way I know to change both overnight is to buy a business that's already performing and plug it into yours.

At 25, with no money and no experience, I bought my first company with the seller financing 100% of the price. There was no bank, no guarantee and no security, and my second deal was the same.

The deal is the easy part. The structure and the people who run it are the whole game.

Doing $1M+ a year and done with the cope? DM me "OPERATOR".

## A3 · Private equity is not coming to save you

Owners love to believe private equity has some secret.

Then you see the inside of a PE-backed roll-up and realise most of them are winging it with other people's money.

I spoke to a lender today funding one in healthcare. The fund is tapped out. Four clinics shut. The GM is on a huge salary and hasn't hit a target. The meetings are all culture and zero numbers.

The lender is the one funding it now, and he's had to get his hands dirty to save his own money.

Here's the lesson for you.

Private equity's edge was never intelligence. It was the playbook: buy businesses with other people's money, structure each deal so the target's profit pays for it, and put operators in charge.

The ones who forget the last part are closing clinics.

You don't need a fund to run that playbook. You need a seller willing to finance the deal, a structure that passes the test (the profit covers the repayments at least one and a half times), and a board around you that has done it before.

I did my first two deals that way: both 100% seller financed, no guarantees, no security.

The difference between being the platform and being the cautionary tale isn't money. It's who's in the room.

## A4 · Most roll-ups die from bad operators

Most acquisitions don't die from a bad price. They die from a bad operator.

A private-equity healthcare roll-up I just heard about is the textbook case: four clinics closed, an overpaid GM, a fund with nothing left, and leadership meetings full of "culture" while the cash ran out. The lender is now funding it and trying to work out how to get his money back.

Every owner thinking about buying a business needs to sit with that.

Buying the company is step one. The first hundred days decide whether you become the platform or the warning.

Before my first acquisition I recruited a board of people who'd actually run healthcare businesses, including a director who'd built fitness businesses to eight figures. They covered leadership, operations, compliance, hiring and leases: everything I didn't know.

That deal was 100% seller financed with a 9-month payment holiday. It paid me about $150k in its first year. My second followed on the same principle.

The money is out there. Sellers will finance you. Lenders will fund you. That lender is literally funding a failing roll-up right now because nobody else will run it properly.

What's scarce is people who can run what they buy.
