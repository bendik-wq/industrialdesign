## H8 · The Payment Holiday

*For $1M–$10M owners who think buying takes a war chest. Picture: a calendar, twelve months crossed out, each page stamped "$0".*

▸ CONTENT

The seller financed the whole price.

And didn't ask for a single repayment for twelve months.

There's no trick in that. It's a structure, and owners have sold businesses this way for as long as businesses have been sold.

▸ HOPE

You've been told that buying a business takes a war chest.

A big deposit, a bank that wants your house, and years of saving before you're allowed to play.

So every year you grow the slow way instead: another campaign, another hire, another location that loses money before it earns any.

That's buying customers at retail, one at a time, with your own cash.

There's a wholesale price.

Picture an owner who has spent thirty years building a business, whose kids don't want it, and whose accountant keeps asking about succession.

A cheque on day one isn't what matters most to them.

What they need is to know the staff and the name will be looked after, and that they'll be paid.

So they finance the sale themselves, and the buyer pays them over time, out of the profits of the business they sold.

And because a new owner needs time to settle in, the first twelve months carry no repayments at all.

Run the numbers on a business making six hundred thousand dollars a year in profit. (illustrative)

Priced at three times earnings, the seller finances one point eight million, at four percent, over seven years.

In year one you pay nothing, and all six hundred thousand stays in the business while you take over.

From year two the repayments run about three hundred and fifty-seven thousand a year, and the profit covers them one point seven times.

The business pays for itself, with money left over.

▸ VALUE

Josh Li bought his first two companies with the seller financing one hundred percent of the price.

He started unemployed, with no experience, after about a thousand cold calls.

He built a multi seven-figure healthcare group on those deals.

A seller agrees to finance when they can feel three things: a team behind the buyer, a price the profit can carry, and a real plan for the handover.

Even the legal and accounting fees can come out of a working-capital line drawn at settlement.

▸ IMPULSE

Not every seller will do it, and not every price works.

At four times earnings, that same deal fails the test, and we'd walk away from it.

Somewhere near you, an owner is deciding right now who gets thirty years of their work.

They'll finance the buyer they trust most.

Smaller than that? Grow the core business first. This isn't for you yet.

## H13 · Same Business, Two Prices

*For owners who've done deals that disappoint, and owners who think acquisitions are gambling. Picture: two identical shopfronts, one lit, one dark.*

▸ CONTENT

Two identical businesses, on the same street, with the same customers and the same six hundred thousand dollars a year in profit.

One will make its buyer wealthy.

The other will sink them.

The only difference is the price, and how it's paid.

▸ HOPE

Most owners believe a good business is a good deal.

Banks know better.

A bank doesn't ask whether the business is good. It asks whether the profit covers the repayments, with room to spare.

Our line is one and a half times.

Buy the first business at three times its earnings, with the seller financing the price over seven years, and the profit covers the repayments one point seven times. It passes. (illustrative)

Buy the second at four times, on the same terms, and cover falls to one point three. It fails, and the bank is right to say no.

Nothing about the business changed. The structure did.

That's how a healthcare group we sat down with lost money on its last ten acquisitions. †

The businesses were fine. The debt was sized to the price instead of the profit, and every site ran on the owner instead of a system.

And it's why owners who've never bought anything think acquisitions are gambling.

Structure turns the gamble into arithmetic.

Change the shape, and the four-times deal works too.

Put sixty percent on bank debt against the business's own cash flow, and let the seller roll forty percent into equity.

Repayments drop to about two hundred and ninety-six thousand a year, and cover climbs above two times. (illustrative)

The seller stays invested in the handover, and gets paid a second time when the group sells.

▸ VALUE

Josh Li built a multi seven-figure healthcare group on this discipline, starting with two deals that were both one hundred percent seller financed.

Every offer is tested before it's made, and a deal that fails the test never gets signed.

That's the whole risk management.

▸ IMPULSE

If you've done deals and the numbers disappoint, the idea was never wrong. The structure was.

Owners who've never bought a business were never taught this part, and it's the only part that matters.

Run a profitable business doing a million dollars a year or more? Apply below, and our team books your strategy call if it's a fit.

*For $10M–$100M operators. Picture: a regional map, pins flipping one by one to a single colour.*

Private equity is rolling up your industry right now.

The platform, or the add-on.

▸ HOPE

In the first five months of 2025, private-equity add-on deals for HVAC companies were up eighty-eight percent on the year before.

Heartland Dental passed two thousand offices, and one acquisition alone added sixty of them.

Wherever a market is fragmented and its owners are getting older, the same play runs.

And the owners are getting older. McKinsey expects about six million small and mid-size businesses to change hands by 2035.

The add-on gets bought at a small-business multiple, three to five times earnings.

The platform gets valued at eight to twelve. (illustrative ranges)

## H21 · Buy at Four, Valued at Eight

*For $10M–$100M operators. Picture: two price tags on the same building, one small, one enormous.*

▸ CONTENT

Buy at four times earnings.

Get valued at eight.

That's what private equity is doing with your competitors, and it's one of the oldest plays in business.

▸ HOPE

In 1901, J.P. Morgan bought Carnegie Steel for about four hundred and eighty million dollars.

He merged it with other steelmakers into U.S. Steel, capitalised at one point four billion: the first billion-dollar company in history.

Morgan understood something most owners still don't. Size changes what a business is worth.

The gap still exists today, at every scale.

A small service business sells for three to five times what it earns, and a group of them is valued at eight to twelve. (illustrative ranges)

Buyers pay more for size, because a group survives when one owner leaves, one site has a bad year, or one customer walks.

You've spent years trying to lift your profit a percent at a time.

The fastest way to raise what your business is worth is to make it bigger, by buying.

▸ VALUE

The arithmetic is simple. (illustrative)

Buy five businesses earning a million a year each, at four times earnings, for twenty million.

The bank lends sixty percent against their cash flow, and the sellers roll forty percent into equity and stay invested in the handover.

The profit covers the bank more than twice over, and none of your own cash goes into the price.

Put them under one roof, and at eight times that five million of profit is worth forty million. At ten, it's fifty.

Twenty million of value, created by size instead of longer hours.

Josh Li built a multi seven-figure healthcare group on this model, starting with two deals that were both one hundred percent seller financed.

▸ IMPULSE

Private equity built an entire industry on this gap.

Nothing says only they get to use it.

The only question is whether you buy at four, or sell at four to someone who does.
